General & Background Information

What is the purpose of course evaluation?

Course evaluation provides concrete suggestions for the improvement of teaching and learning situations and enables the University of Bern to maintain a high level of teaching quality and initiate its further development. In addition, it serves as evidence of quality for legislators and the public. You can find more about the purposes of course evaluation here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#e1121856).

Which courses and assessments are evaluated?

The quality officers of the faculties are responsible for the selection and registration of courses and assessments to be evaluated. The minimum requirements according to the framework concept are:
- Every course should be evaluated at least once every three years
- 50% of all assessments should be evaluated at least once every 3 years
- For new lecturers and those in qualifying positions, a final evaluation is mandatory
Read more about mandatory evaluations and cycles here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933).

How often are evaluations carried out?

- Midterm evaluation: no prescribed cycle
- Final evaluation: every course should be evaluated at least once every three years
- Evaluation of assessments: 50% of all assessments should be evaluated at least once within 3 years
Read more about this here: Cycle (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933); timing of evaluations (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121958).

What do the questionnaires look like?

Sample questionnaires for the midterm evaluation, final evaluation, and assessment evaluation can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#section1122024). Samples of the additional modules (optional extra question blocks) can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1101959).

Is it possible to customize a questionnaire?

Customizing the standard questionnaires is not possible. For the final evaluation, optional additional question blocks (modules) are available, which can be added to the core questionnaire as needed. The sample additional modules can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1101959). Faculties may also design their own survey instruments. The Unit for Course Evaluation can provide feedback on custom-designed questionnaires, but implementation and analysis cannot be done centrally. Custom questionnaires do not replace the mandatory final evaluation using the standard questionnaire.

In which languages are the questionnaires available?

The questionnaires for midterm, final evaluations, and assessment evaluation are available in German, English, and French (language can be selected via the flag at the top of the survey).

How many students must participate in a survey at minimum?

To preserve student anonymity, survey results are only sent automatically if at least 5 students have participated. In exceptional cases, lecturers may receive results upon request if fewer than 5 but more than 2 students participated. The results of assessment evaluations are only sent when at least 5 students have participated.

Why should students participate in the surveys?

Course evaluation is a tool to measure to what extent the conditions and target criteria for quality teaching have been fulfilled. This measurement is based on students’ ratings in the surveys and is thus only possible through student participation. Students benefit in two ways:
- Lecturers and the university use survey results to further develop teaching. Resulting improvements benefit students in their courses and assessments.
- Surveys give students an opportunity to reflect on their own behavior and contribution to learning success, and improve it, if necessary, in the future.
Full participation in an evaluation ensures more representative results. Read more about the purposes of evaluation here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

How can students participate in a survey?

Final and midterm evaluation: Lecturers provide a QR code (typically during the second to last session), leading to the survey. Alternatively, a survey link may be distributed (e.g., by email or ILIAS).
Assessment evaluation: Students receive a survey link via email from the Unit for Course Evaluation.

Why do students have to participate in so many surveys?

The faculty quality officers determine which and how many courses and assessments are evaluated each semester. Some faculties evaluate a large number or even all courses and assessments, so students may have to fill out many surveys. Read more about the frequency and cycle of evaluations here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933).

Are the surveys anonymous?

Yes, all surveys are anonymous. It is not possible to trace who gave which answer.

Questions about Preparing for an Evaluation

What is new regarding the project to digitalize course evaluation?

All information about the digitalization project, instructions, and training materials can be found on the intranet (access for UniBE employees only): Link (https://intern.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/lehre/lehrevaluation/digitalisierung_evaluation/schulungen_und_unterlagen/index_ger.html)

Is there a deadline for evaluating a course?

The following registration deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html) apply for registration. There are no specific deadlines for implementation, as it depends on the timing of each course or assessment. However, surveys may only be extended up to two weeks after the end of the semester so results can be shared promptly with faculties. For final evaluation, surveys should be conducted at a time allowing for discussion of results with students (e.g., third-to-last or second-to-last session).

How can a course or exam be registered for evaluation?

Registration is done via KSL - the core system for teaching - left tab "Dozierende obligatorisch". Usually, a central office in the faculty or institute arranges the registration. In exceptional cases, lecturers themselves may register (especially for voluntary evaluations). An overview of registration modes and deadlines can be found under Deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html).

Why aren’t evaluation registrations in KSL automatically carried over to the next semester?

There are no automatic processes to avoid administrative oversights. At the start of a semester, a deliberate decision for evaluation should be taken by a responsible individual. Please note the minimum requirements for the cycle (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933).

For a course with multiple lecturers, are surveys completed for each person or jointly for the whole course?

That depends on how the lecturers taught. If instructors taught independently, it makes sense to obtain individual feedback. In this case, the relevant instructors must be selected in KSL, so that a separate survey is created for each. Note: students will have to complete separate surveys for each individually evaluated person, so a maximum of three instructors per course should be evaluated individually. If instructors worked as a team, the "Team evaluation" option may be chosen to get feedback for the whole team. For assessments: one survey per assessment is created, either as an individual survey for the main responsible person, or a team evaluation if several instructors share responsibility.

Should evaluation be online or with paper questionnaires?

Since the Spring semester 2022, all surveys (final, midterm, and assessment evaluations) are conducted online. Advantages include:
- No printing or scanning effort for lecturers/admin staff
- Results are provided automatically and immediately after the survey closes
- Ability to offer multiple language versions and include filter questions
- Automatic reminder emails are possible for surveys with personalized TAN links
- Sustainability: about 100,000 sheets of paper are saved each semester through online surveys

Which survey methods are available for course evaluation?

There is a distinction between surveys with personalized links and non-personalized links. Midterm and final evaluations are generally conducted with non-personalized links (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1127465) ("Losung"). Surveys for assessment evaluation are always conducted with personalized links (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/evaluation_der_leistungskontrolle/index_ger.html#pane1065968) ("TAN").

When are the surveys conducted?

Survey dates depend on the type of evaluation (midterm, final, or assessment evaluation) and the individual schedule of the course and exams. How can I change the date of an online survey?

If you have a desired date for conducting an online survey, you can specify it when registering in KSL. If you need to change the date after setting up the evaluation, inform the Unit for Course Evaluation (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch) by email or use this form (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/formular_onlineevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html).

How can the survey language be selected?

The questionnaires are available in German, English, and French (final, midterm, and assessment evaluations). When registering an evaluation in KSL, the language does not need to be selected. Students can set the desired language in the survey by clicking the relevant country flag. Reports for lecturers can be produced in German, English, or French (default is German). If you need reports in English or French, please let the Unit for Course Evaluation (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch) know.

Why was no survey set up for a course?

If no survey was set up, the course was probably not registered for evaluation in KSL. Please contact the Unit for Course Evaluation to see if a survey can be set up retroactively.

Which students are eligible to participate in an online survey?

Final and midterm evaluations: The lecturer distributes the survey link to eligible students by email, publishes it on ILIAS, or includes the QR code in the presentation.
Assessment evaluation: Students eligible to participate are those who receive an invitation email (these are students registered for the exam, according to the participant list from KSL).

Can a course be evaluated if there are fewer than 5 participants?

Standard surveys are recommended for courses with 5 or more participants. For data protection reasons, results from surveys (midterm or final) with fewer than 5 participants are only provided on request. Additionally, only survey results from 5 or more participants are included in the faculty average. It is possible to create surveys for smaller courses, but results may not be viewable. Assessments are only evaluated via the standardized method from 5 participants. For exams with fewer than 5 participants, no results are provided. For exams with very few participants, faculties should use other, qualitative evaluation methods.

How can you tell which lecturers and which course are being evaluated with a given questionnaire?

The header of the online survey and the report state which course and which lecturers the survey concerns. If multiple lecturers are evaluated together, this is also noted in the header. (Exception: if there are more than five lecturers, only the main lecturer followed by "et al." is listed due to space reasons.) See examples here (https://scanserveruls.unibe.ch/evasys/online.php?p=2FMEV): Sample survey; sample report (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1009020/e1009037/Muster_Schlusseval_Report_Deutsch_Website_20240125_ger.pdf).

Who receives the results of a survey?

Detailed results are usually sent only to the lecturers included in the evaluation. In some institutes, the report is also sent to the person responsible for evaluation. At the end of an evaluation period, aggregated results for the entire faculty are sent to the faculty quality officers.

How and when do lecturers receive the evaluation results?

Final or midterm evaluation: Lecturers receive the evaluation result immediately after the survey closes. A PDF report with automatic analysis and students’ comments is sent by email. In addition, an interactive HTML report can be accessed via a link in the email. If the report is not received in the expected time, contact the Unit for Course Evaluation. Assessment evaluation: The PDF report is only sent after the grades are confirmed. Read more here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/evaluation_der_leistungskontrolle/index_ger.html#pane1065968). Example PDF reports can be viewed here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#section1122015).

Where can students view evaluation results?

The Unit for Course Evaluation does not provide students with access to results. However, lecturers should discuss the results and any consequential improvements with students where possible. Faculties can publish aggregated results through their own channels (e.g., their websites). More about closing the feedback loop is available here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1122010).

Are evaluation results published?

The Unit for Course Evaluation does not publish results. Some faculties may publish internal results on their respective channels.

Should the results be discussed with students?

To close the first feedback loop, the framework concept provides that lecturers should discuss survey results and any improvements before the end of the semester. This way, students know lecturers are engaging with feedback.

How do I interpret the results report?

The detailed report for the final evaluation contains the overall result, allowing you to determine what success level (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121983) the result achieved overall. It also contains individual results for the indicators “learning progress” and “satisfaction,” and results per question (distribution of answers, mean, and standard deviation for scale questions). Additionally, the detailed report shows via comparison profile lines how the results relate to the previous year's faculty average (spring or fall semester). Afterwards, the responses to open questions are listed.
Results from midterm evaluations and assessment evaluations are not combined into an overall result. These should be interpreted per question or indicator, in the context of the course or assessment. The Unit for Course Evaluation is available for help interpreting results. Sample reports can be seen here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#section1122015).

What are the consequences of positive results?

Final evaluations that achieve the “outstanding” level (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121983) are nominated for the ALL Award (Recognition of Outstanding Teaching Achievement) (https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch/foerderangebote/all/). Faculties may use additional criteria (https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch/foerderangebote/all/#pane1420600) to decide which of these courses receive the award.

What are the consequences of negative results?

Measures for insufficient final evaluation results are specified in the framework concept (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1009019/e1009041/Rahmenkonzept_2024_Final_ger.pdf). Faculties may also define additional actions in their own QSE guidelines. The minimum requirements can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/massnahmen/index_ger.html).

Lecturers have access to a comprehensive higher education didactics offering from the Learning & Development department (https://lead.unibe.ch/index_ger.html) to systematically optimize their teaching. Another way to improve quality is to conduct a midterm evaluation (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/zwischenevaluation/index_ger.html) during the semester, which provides formative feedback and specific suggestions for improvement by identifying potential weaknesses in teaching. Interim Feedback  
How are your students coping with the course? What difficulties in understanding are there? What can be changed to promote student learning?

With interim feedback, you’ll find answers to questions about the further development of your teaching.

Background

Quality Assurance (https://lead.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/zwischenfeedback/index_ger.html#collapse_pane971338)

Course evaluations are an integral part of the quality assurance and quality development concept at the University of Bern. Regularly, so-called midterm evaluations, final evaluations, and evaluations of performance assessments are carried out with standardized tools. In order to acknowledge and promote formative evaluation, voluntary interim feedback has been incorporated as an additional component into the university’s framework concept for course evaluations (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html). The interim feedback does not follow a defined process and there are no specifications. It is a quick and uncomplicated form of feedback, usually requiring little preparation.

Benefits  
Interim feedback is important for the development of teaching and for student learning for three reasons:

- As instructors, you are free to choose which aspects of teaching and learning you want to focus on in interim feedback.
- Adjustments to teaching based on interim feedback can have a motivating effect on the engagement of your students.
- If you take up questions from the final evaluation during the interim feedback and discuss them with the students, this can have a positive impact on the evaluation culture and the results of the final evaluation.

Planning and Implementation  
Timing  
We recommend that you collect interim feedback no later than the second third of the semester, so that the results can be discussed with students and any necessary adjustments can still be made during the current course. For specific feedback, e.g., on a certain method or a concrete input, a later time during the semester is also conceivable.

Analysis  
The main advantage of interim feedback is the ability to ask students directly for their input and obtain concrete suggestions. Above all, let the students know which points of the feedback caught your attention, which aspects were helpful to you, and which you will use as a basis for making changes. It is often very helpful to point out what cannot be changed for didactic reasons. In this way, encourage students to reflect on their own contribution to the success of the course and to their own learning.

more (https://lead.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/zwischenfeedback/muddiest_point/index_ger.html) Muddiest Point  
At the end of a lesson, ask your students to write down those aspects that are still the most unclear to them, for example with the question: "What was least clear to you in this session?" The students record their “muddiest points” and hand them in anonymously. You review and cluster these and, at the next opportunity, address the points that were mentioned most frequently by the students.

Digital alternative: In synchronous phases, muddiest points can easily be implemented online using so-called Audience Response Systems (ARS) such as ILIAS-LiveVoting (https://ilias.unibe.ch/goto_ilias3_unibe_file_700326_download.html) (open-ended question). For implementing muddiest points in asynchronous phases, the ILIAS survey (https://ilias.unibe.ch/goto_ilias3_unibe_file_31731_download.html) is suitable.

Further assessment methods to determine your students’ learning progress can be found here: www.assessment.unibe.ch (http://www.assessment.unibe.ch/). Interim Evaluation

The interim evaluation is student feedback that is collected using a standardized questionnaire. It is a service provided by the Fachstelle LVE and is carried out using the central evaluation system evasys. The interim evaluation allows instructors to collect opinions and suggestions from students at an early stage in the course, so that the course can be improved accordingly during the semester.



Application Possibilities of the Interim Evaluation
VOLUNTARY

The interim evaluation is basically voluntary.

AS FOLLOW-UP

For courses that were rated as "inadequate" in a final evaluation, the interim evaluation is mandatory for the next session of the course.



Purposes of the Interim Evaluation

For more on the purposes of course evaluation, see here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

1) Further development, improvement

The interim evaluation is purely formative, i.e. it is oriented towards improvement. Its purpose is to provide instructors with detailed data-based indications of areas where the course shows potential for improvement (see model of teaching quality (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1027399/e1027402/VRQ_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_md3_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_ger.jpg)).

2) Preparation for the final evaluation

The interim evaluation is a valuable preparation for the final evaluation. Since the interim evaluation specifically addresses the quality dimensions that constitute good teaching, it supports instructors in identifying potential for improvement. If measures to optimize these dimensions are subsequently taken, this will be reflected in the next final evaluation, as its content builds on the interim evaluation.

3) Promotion of feedback culture

Furthermore, the interim evaluation can promote an open feedback culture within a course.

Please note that an interim evaluation does not replace the final evaluation.



Timing of the Interim Evaluation

Standard time for implementation: 1st half of the course sessions, at the latest by the 9th week of the semester (depending on the course dates)

Survey Method

The surveys for the interim evaluation are conducted online with a non-personalized link (token). The link and the QR code are sent to the main instructor or, in some cases, to an administrative office in the faculty, and then made available to students.



Content, Languages

CONTENT

The instrument for the interim evaluation is based on the model of teaching quality (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1027399/e1027402/VRQ_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_md3_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_ger.jpg) and is structured as follows:

- Dimension Instructors (presentation of the topic, specification of learning outcomes, interaction with students, learning progress-related feedback)
- Dimension Course (content and structure, support for learning through resources, level of challenge: over- or under-demand, pace)
- Dimension Students (active participation, rhythmic/regular learning)

More information here under Content, Languages (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/zwischenevaluation/index_ger.html#collapse_pane1065609)


LANGUAGES

The questionnaire is available in German, English, and French (selection via the flag icon in the survey).



Process of the Interim Evaluation

1. Planning  
In an initial step, instructors plan 2-3 dates for the survey during course preparation: one date for conducting the survey, possibly a fallback date, and a date for discussing the results with the students.

2. Registration  
Registration is usually done by the 3rd week of the semester in KSL (see deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/zwischenevaluation/%7B$uid:b3445c2b-6af9-4547-80b3-39a8769286b2%7D)). Register the interim evaluation in KSL.

Courses for which a mandatory interim evaluation is required due to a negative final evaluation are registered automatically or by a central office of the institute.

3. Creation of the survey  
Four weeks before the midpoint of the course (or immediately, if registration takes place later), registrations are transferred to the evaluation system evasys and the surveys are set up.

4. Sending the link  
An email with the link and QR code to the survey is sent 1–3 weeks before the planned closing date of the interim evaluation to the main responsible instructor or, on request, to alternative recipients.

This email contains the planned closing date of the survey as well as further information about its implementation.

5. Conducting the survey  
The interim evaluation should be conducted in the first half of the course sessions.

As soon as the responsible person receives the link or the QR code, the survey can be conducted. Ideally, about 10 minutes are provided to students at the start of a course session.

For higher response rates, it is recommended to conduct the survey in the (possibly virtual) presence of the instructor(s).

The survey is automatically closed on the date provided at registration or on a date set by the specialist office.

6. Report  
Immediately after the end of the survey, all instructors of the evaluated course receive an automatic evaluation report, provided the minimum response rate of 5 replies is reached.

If there are fewer than 5 responses, instructors receive an automatic message stating that the report could not be generated. Upon request to the Fachstelle LVE (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch), the office can provide the report or at least the responses to open questions manually, depending on the response rate.

For questions regarding interpretation of the results, instructors can contact the Fachstelle LVE (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch).

For questions on higher education didactics, the department LEAD (https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/hochschuldidaktik_und_lehrentwicklung/) provides advisory support to instructors.

7. Discussion  
It is recommended to discuss the survey results, as well as the insights and possible consequences (e.g., improvement measures), with the students.

8. Adjustments  
Student feedback may provide an opportunity to make adjustments to the course.

9. Follow-up  
Course evaluation is designed in accordance with the model of teaching quality (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121880) so that the questionnaires for interim and final evaluation are content-aligned. This means that a final evaluation following an interim evaluation can indicate how successfully improvement measures have been implemented in the course. It is therefore recommended to use interim and final evaluations in combination.

Thanks to interim tests, students receive feedback about their level of learning, and you as the instructor receive information about what the students have already understood well and what may need to be repeated. Interim tests can be used both during synchronous phases (e.g., a brief review of the key points from the last lecture) and during asynchronous phases (e.g., as part of inverted classroom scenarios) of the course. REGULAR

The framework concept stipulates that 50% of all performance assessments are evaluated regularly (at least every 3 years). Faculties plan the evaluations accordingly and select the performance assessments to be evaluated, taking into account the faculty QSE guidelines.

VOLUNTARY

Instructors can voluntarily register their performance assessments for evaluation.

Purposes of the Evaluation of Performance Assessments  
Performance assessments (exams) play a central role in the day-to-day teaching and learning at universities by directing teaching and guiding student learning behavior. For this reason, the evaluation of performance assessments (ELK) is of central value for organizing teaching and teaching development.

The ELK provides instructors and faculty examination committees with data-based suggestions for what can be improved in performance assessments—and additionally in courses (improvement orientation). The ELK also serves as an accountability tool for legislators and the public.

More information on the purposes of evaluation can be found here: (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

Timing of the Evaluation of Performance Assessments  
Standard timing: After the performance assessment has taken place (usually at 20:00 on the day of the exam), starting from the 12th week of the semester.

Surveys are usually conducted before grades are announced, unless otherwise agreed. Evaluation results are only provided to instructors after grading is complete. Particular attention is paid to data protection compliance.

Process of Performance Assessment Evaluation  
1. Registration  
Evaluations should be registered in KSL by the 10th week of the semester (see deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html#e855666)). Registration is usually completed by a central office of the institute.

To register an evaluation of a performance assessment in KSL, the following information is required:
- Exam date(s)
- Exam registration and deregistration deadlines

It is possible to register several exam dates for evaluation, but it is recommended to register only one date per performance assessment.

Early evaluations, which are to be conducted before the standard implementation period (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html) must, in addition to registration in KSL, be confirmed to the LVE office via email.

Participant lists with at least 5 students should be available at the respective exam date. They can also be saved in KSL at a later time, but not later than one day before the exam. Performance assessment evaluations with fewer than 5 registrations will not be set up in the evaluation system.

2. Creation of Surveys  
After the end of the registration period, the LVE office prepares the surveys for the ELK.

3. Information  
Instructors receive an automatic email about the start of the survey as soon as students have received the link to the survey.

The LVE office does not inform instructors in advance that an evaluation is planned.

4. Data Collection  
At 20:00 on the day of the performance assessment, students receive an email with the access link to the questionnaire.

The surveys remain open for 24 hours (oral performance assessments) or 4 days (written performance assessments and all other types) and are closed automatically. For surveys that last 4 days, a reminder is sent after 2 days to those students who have not yet completed the survey.

5. Announcement of Grades  
After the survey is closed, the instructors or persons responsible can enter the grades in KSL and forward them to the dean's office for approval.

Instructors are free to determine the grades earlier, but they should not be communicated to the students yet (unless other arrangements have been made with the LVE office or in exceptional cases—e.g. if exchange students need their grades earlier).

Subsequently, instructors confirm to the LVE office via email that the grades have been entered and forwarded to the dean's office.

6. Validity Check  
Once the LVE office has received confirmation of grade entry, it checks the response rate and the number of valid answers to ensure student anonymity.

7. Report  
If the response rate is sufficient (at least 5 valid responses), the report is delivered to the instructors. If the response rate is too low, the report is not generated.

If confirmation of grade announcement is missing, the reports will only be sent to the instructors after the deadlines individually agreed with the faculties (e.g., 6 weeks after the last exam).

8. Sharing the Results  
It is recommended to share the evaluation results and insights gained with the students (e.g. via ILIAS or by email).

9. Adjustments  
If necessary, instructors make adjustments to the didactic concept of the course and/or the performance assessment.

10. Follow-up  
The faculty examination committee or a commission/body fulfilling this role analyzes the results, develops recommendations, and takes appropriate measures.

The results of the ELK may also be incorporated into the evaluation of study programs/courses.

Framework Concept  
The Framework Concept (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1009019/e1009041/Rahmenkonzept_2024_Final_ger.pdf) for the implementation and use of course evaluations provides an overview of the measures for quality assurance and development (QSE) at the level of courses. This page describes the key points of the framework concept.

Purposes of the Evaluations  
Course evaluations at the University of Bern serve multiple purposes. They are conducted in the form of student surveys, thus including students in the design of teaching (co-determination). The data collected provides concrete guidance for improving teaching and learning situations (improvement focus). Finally, it serves as proof of quality for the legislators and the public (accountability).

1) Proof of Quality (Accountability)  

Since universities fulfill a publicly funded and legally regulated educational mission, they are obliged to make the implementation and achievement of the objectives of their services (in this case, the courses) transparent to the legislators and the public.

The evaluation of courses is a key part of a university's quality assurance and development system and thus a prerequisite for successful institutional accreditation. Only accredited tertiary institutions may continue to call themselves “universities” and qualify for federal contributions.

A functioning system for the evaluation of courses and achievement controls is required by quality standards 3.2 and 3.4 of the Higher Education Funding and Coordination Law (HFKG) (https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2014/691/de).

In addition, the evaluation serves internal monitoring and accountability for the university management.

More on this:

Accreditation at UniBE (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/institutionelle_akkreditierung/index_ger.html)  
Swiss Accreditation Council SAR (https://akkreditierungsrat.ch/akkreditierung/)  
Quality Standards HFKG for Institutional Accreditation (https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2015/362/de#sec_6)

2) Further Development (Improvement Focus)  

The evaluation results serve the further development of teaching. The aim here is to identify strengths and weaknesses of the teaching in order to generate ideas for modifications.

The results of the evaluations (interim, final, and evaluation of achievement controls) are used to improve teaching on two levels:

- The lecturers use the results to further develop their own concept (learning outcomes, teaching/learning activities, exams).
- The faculties and institutes use the results to optimize the prerequisites and framework for good teaching.

3) Co-determination  

By participating in the surveys, students have the opportunity to communicate their opinions about the courses and achievement controls and are thus involved in shaping teaching. Co-determination is anchored in the quality standards for institutional accreditation (Quality Standard 1.3).

Model of Teaching Quality  
In order for course evaluation to fulfill its purposes, it must answer the following question:

To what extent is good teaching implemented in the courses at the University of Bern?

But what does "good teaching" mean?

Based on findings from teaching and learning research, teaching is considered “good” when three key factors are well coordinated (“didactic coherence”, English: “constructive alignment”):

- the intended learning outcomes
- the teaching and learning activities
- the achievement controls

Drawing on these findings and the multidimensional model of teaching quality by Rindermann (2009), a model of teaching quality was developed at the University of Bern. The model shows the dimensions relevant to the quality of a course and an achievement control, defines the target criteria and their relationships (see figure).

The model is the basis for our survey instruments.

The data collected in the surveys indicate in which areas there is potential for improvement for the respective course and/or achievement control.

More information under Model of Teaching Quality here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#collapse_pane1121880)

Methodology, Sample Questionnaires, Content and Intentions  
Methodology  
Course evaluation at the University of Bern is based on student feedback, a validated means for assessing course quality (e.g. Treischl & Wolbring, 2017; Fondel et al., 2015; Spooren et al., 2013; Marsh, 2007).

Frequency of Evaluations  
The evaluations follow a regular cycle defined in the framework concept:

Final Evaluation

The minimum requirement is to evaluate each course at least once every three years. Within this framework, each faculty plans its own evaluations according to its own QSE guidelines (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/index_ger.html#e718012) and monitors compliance. For new types of courses and newly appointed lecturers, the final evaluation is mandatory, as it is for those on qualifying posts (e.g. assistant professorships with tenure track). Courses that were rated "inadequate" in the previous evaluation must be evaluated again the next time.

Evaluation of Achievement Controls

The minimum requirement stipulates that 50% of all achievement controls must be evaluated at least once every 3 years. The faculties plan these evaluations within this framework and select which achievement controls to evaluate considering their QSE guidelines (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/index_ger.html#e718012).

Interim Evaluation

For courses rated "inadequate" in the previous evaluation, an interim evaluation must also be conducted the next time.

Lecturers may also voluntarily sign up their course for interim, final, or achievement control evaluations.

Data Collection Times  
Evaluations are conducted at three different points in the semester.

For registration deadlines and exact implementation periods for each semester, see Deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html).

Interim Evaluation

- Registration deadline: End of week 3 of the semester
- Standard implementation period: First half of the course dates, at latest by week 9 (depending on course schedule)

Final Evaluation

- Registration deadline: End of week 8 of the semester
- Standard implementation period: Towards the end of the course, from week 11

Evaluation of Achievement Controls

- Registration deadline: End of week 10 of the semester
- Standard implementation period: from week 12, after the achievement control has taken place (usually at 20:00 on the day of the exam, unless otherwise agreed — before the grade is announced)

Criteria and Determination of Success Levels, Consequences of Categorization  
The results of the final evaluation are assigned to one of five success levels. The assignment is based on the overall result from a final evaluation. Mean values from questions 1.1 – 1.3 (learning progress) and 2.1 – 2.5 (satisfaction) are considered.

Since FS24, the following success levels and thresholds, defined together with the faculties and an external expert, apply:

- ≥5.7 = "excellent" (level dark green)
- ≥5.5 <5.7 = "very good" (level light green)
- ≥4.5 <5.5 = "good" (level blue)
- ≥4.0 <4.5 = "sufficient" (level yellow)
- <4.0 = "inadequate" (level red)

QSE in Course Evaluation  
The quality assurance and development (QSE) of courses is organized in a cyclical process with three interrelated levels:

First Level: Course  
Lecturers are responsible for QSE at the level of each course. They design the course taking into account previous evaluation results (1). They conduct the course and evaluations and derive suggestions for improving the next course and, if necessary, achievement control (2/3) from the results. Results and planned adjustments (4) are communicated to students whenever possible.

Second Level: Faculty  
Faculties create an evaluation plan (5) and ensure its implementation (6). The QSE officers of the faculties receive aggregated results of final evaluations and achievement control evaluations (7) from the LVE office and take appropriate measures (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/massnahmen/index_ger.html) (8) as specified in the framework concept and faculty quality guidelines.

Third Level: University Management  
At the end of the academic year, the QSE officers of the faculties submit a report (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1124557/Berichterstattung_Vorlage_Semesteruebergreifend_ger.pdf) to the LVE office. They reflect on the evaluation results, set them in context, and report on planned or already implemented QSE measures.

Subsequently, the LVE office prepares a summary report including a brief statement on faculty reports and communicates the results to university management.

The results are discussed as needed in operational or strategy meetings (11). If necessary, specific goals are set and measures are derived (12), which in turn result in adjustments to the entire process and/or the quality strategy (9) and its implementation (10).

You present your students with a brief, clear question that is relevant to the further development of your course, and write it down for all students to see. The students answer this question briefly and concisely (no more than two or three sentences), in any order. As the instructor, you record the responses on a flipchart or the blackboard and summarize them. The Blitzlicht method can also be used as a formative assessment tool to learn more about the students’ level of understanding. The method is suitable for groups of up to 25 students and takes about 10–20 minutes, depending on the group size.

Digital alternative: A Blitzlicht can also be very easily used in Zoom, Teams, or similar platforms. Ideally, results are documented in a protocol on a shared screen. Ampelfeedback  
The students receive green, yellow, and red cards and indicate by raising them their agreement (green card), indecision (yellow card), or rejection (red card) to a question or statement formulated by you. Count the voting results directly and record them in writing in a way that is clearly visible to everyone (e.g., flipchart, blackboard, visualizer). The method can be used with groups of up to 200 students; for groups larger than 30 people, additional “vote counters” are needed. In smaller groups, you can ask individual students to briefly comment on their voting behavior.

Evaluation of Courses and Assessments
Courses constitute a significant part of teaching. With the help of student feedback, several relevant questions about courses can be assessed. All teaching staff's courses are regularly evaluated. The faculties determine the scope and process of evaluation and monitor the procedure. In cooperation with the Vice-Rectorate Teaching, they establish which average values must be achieved and ensure that the results are used to further develop the courses and teaching in general. The university’s higher education pedagogy division supports faculty members in their teaching development.

The following questions are among those considered relevant:

- Presentation of the subject (definition of learning objectives and structuring of the course)
- Engagement of the instructor
- Difficulty and scope of the course
- Students’ learning progress
- Students’ engagement
- Background data

During the semester, but no later than the end of the semester, instructors briefly discuss the results with their students and outline planned improvement measures. Assessments play an important guiding and orientation role in studies. They refer to one or several courses or sections of the study program and are conducted orally or in writing. The criteria for assessing students’ performance must be defined, communicated, and applied systematically, transparently, and consistently. The university ensures that assessments meet these criteria.

1.  
Introduction  
  
Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE) have been regarded as a central element of all university areas at the University of Bern not only since the implementation of the Higher Education Promotion and Coordination Act (HFKG) of 2011. By establishing a comprehensive quality assurance system encompassing all areas of university performance, processes are systematized, and suitable measures for QSE are developed and implemented in all university areas.  
  
Foundations for adapting existing or developing new measures for quality assurance and quality development are the quality strategy of the University of Bern and all overriding strategies and resolutions. The present guideline document implements the requirements of the university evaluation regulations.  
  
  
1.1  
Purpose of the Guidelines  
The document presented focuses on the tasks of the university central administration. Its scope therefore includes all central university units – the Rectorate, the Vice Rectorates, and the Administration Directorate with their respective subunits. The guidelines are addressed to the Quality Officers (Q-Beauftragten) of the central administration as well as those responsible for QSE tasks (Q-Verantwortliche) within the units of the central administration.  
  
The guidelines describe the principles of QSE at the University of Bern for the tasks and services of the university central administration and show how the different elements of QSE are operationalized (organization, communication, etc.). They define framework conditions for the reporting and review of services and tasks of the administrative units.  
  
  
1.2  
Foundations and Principles of the Guidelines  
The HFKG defines the requirements for higher education institutions, and institutional accreditation ensures that institutions develop their own quality assurance systems and conduct quality assurance and development according to nationally and internationally valid standards. The law provides the necessary foundations for a competitive, permeable, and high-quality higher education space in Switzerland.  
  
According to Art. 30, Sec. 1a of the HFKG, a prerequisite for institutional accreditation is that the higher education institution has a quality assurance system that includes university administration. For its implementation, the requirements of the Standards and Guidelines for Quality Assurance in the European Higher Education Area (ESG) as well as the requirements of the university evaluation regulations are taken into account. In accordance with the university strategy 2021, QSE for the university central administration will be expanded.  
  
The guidelines pursue the following principles, valid throughout the university:

Ensuring a competitive scientific institution  
•  
Ensuring and further developing a strong awareness of quality and thereby establishing a university quality culture

•  
Involvement and responsibility of all university members according to their areas of competence

1.3  
QSE Organization at the University of Bern and in the Central Administration  
The Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission) advises the University Management on all matters of quality assurance and quality development. Each of the faculties, the central administration, the mid-level staff, and the students have a Q-Beauftragte (Quality Officer) who is a member of the university-wide QSE Commission. The units organize QSE specifically according to their tasks.  
  
The Q-Beauftragte of the central administration is a member of the university's QSE Commission, organizes and coordinates the QSE-relevant measures and tasks of the university central administration, and is responsible for reporting to the University Management. In the respective units of the central administration, the Q-Verantwortliche coordinates information and data relevant for reporting, and, on behalf of the respective management and in agreement with the Q-Beauftragte, organizes regularly conducted evaluation procedures (see Chapter 2.1).

It is tasked, among other things, with developing and regularly updating action plans in collaboration with the responsible units, faculties, and competence centers. The members of AKKRED have a specific view of ongoing tasks and projects, especially of the various areas defined in the university quality strategy 2018–2021 (Figure 2) (e.g., strategic implementation of the teaching university, support for early-career researchers) and are responsible for the annual QSE reporting (see Chapter 2.2). The working group is composed of staff members from the central administration and is led and coordinated by the QSE Department in the Vice Rectorate Quality.

Control and Reporting  
  
The central elements of the university QSE system follow the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle. The PDCA cycle describes a continuous development process and is described in more detail in the university quality strategy. The quality cycle is linked to the management of the central administration units. The management of the units is the responsibility of the University Management.

2.1  
Control and QSE Instruments for the Central Administration  
For the purpose of internal management, the respective major services of the central administration units are defined by the departmental managements together with the University Management and set out as service descriptions. In this way, the university ensures that it can respond flexibly and differentially to the demands placed on it.  
  
Based on the individual tasks and measures formulated in the university action plan, the state and implementation of these are reported to the University Management every four years by the departmental managements, supported by the Q-Verantwortlichen, in the form of self-evaluation reports. At the start of a new four-year period, the service descriptions are updated on the basis of the self-evaluation reports. The units of the central administration are given considerable leeway with this model; at the same time, it provides the opportunity to analyze and evaluate the services provided, as well as to reconsider whether services and measures should be removed, supplemented, or newly introduced.  
  
Furthermore, on behalf of the Senate, the appropriateness and performance of the existing organizational structures in the central administration are assessed by means of a peer review/external evaluation every eight years, with regard to the fulfillment of tasks, resources, and the efficiency of processes.1  
  
The units of the central administration describe and document their regularly performed, standardized activities and services in the form of process modeling, and save these in the electronic process documentation. Through an annual process review, the documentation is kept up to date (see below). The responsibility for coordinating the process review is determined by the Q-Beauftragte.

Elements of Annual Reporting

•

In the QSE Department, the information relevant for the management and governance of the university is filtered, confirmed by the Q-Beauftragte, and prepared for discussion at an annual meeting of the University Management. Following the traffic light system provided for in the action plan, the implementation of the objectives, measures, and services of the units in the central administration is addressed. Measures assessed as insufficient or not fulfilled, or as necessary to be supplemented, are included in the revised version of the action plan.  
•

The modeled processes are reviewed and, if necessary, updated annually by the units in a review procedure. The position entrusted by the Q-Beauftragte with this task reports to the Q-Beauftragte of the central administration after the completion of the annual review on the basis of the process documentation.

1. Introduction  
1.1 Fundamentals and Principles  
The University of Bern is obliged, on the basis of national legislation, to ensure and document the quality assurance and development (QSE) of its core tasks of teaching, research, and further education.1  
Regularly conducted evaluations2 of study programs and courses of study3 are an integral part of the system for QSE of teaching at the University of Bern. They are used to determine and document the quality of study offerings and content, their topicality and strategic relevance, as well as options for their further development.  
Planning, design, implementation, and evaluation of the evaluations are the responsibility of the faculties and the evaluating departments and institutes, which are guided by the university standards and guidelines.4  
Study Program Evaluations (SPE)5 are primarily an instrument of QSE, enabling systematic reflection on the quality of study offerings at the level of the evaluating departments and institutes from different perspectives. In these, quantitatively and qualitatively collected data and information on the structures, content concepts, and organization of the study programs and courses of study are processed. Thus, the evaluations serve the goal of providing the best possible education for students by helping to further develop and, if necessary, to correct study content and conditions; additionally, they can contribute to sharpening the profile of a subject, field, or university institute.  
Furthermore, the evaluations are important for the development and positioning of the University as a teaching institution, as well as for the faculties, departments, and institutes. In strategic discussions between University Management and the faculties, or in structural reporting processes, evaluation results may play a role as planning elements. 

1.2 This Document  
To support those responsible for QSE in the faculties and the evaluating departments and institutes, the present recommendations and guidance are made available for carrying out evaluations of study programs and courses of study. This document was prepared in close collaboration with staff from the Department of Evaluation at the Center for University Continuing Education, the Vice-Rectorate (VR) Teaching, as well as the faculty-based Scientific Staff for Quality (WiMa_Q)6 and replaces the instructions for the evaluation of study programs at the University of Bern approved in October 2018.  
The document describes the framework conditions and objectives of SPE procedures, their process sequence, as well as responsibilities in the evaluation process. The advice and recommendations serve as orientation and are not binding. Nevertheless, they should be taken into account wherever possible in order to ensure that evaluation procedures are successfully conducted. In justified cases, deviation from the outlined procedure is possible, provided that it actually improves the quality of the evaluation or at least does not impair the achievement of the evaluation objectives.  
The guidelines are divided into two separate parts – the descriptive main part and the appendix, in which instruments and methods related to the conduct of the SPE are described.7 This division is intended to increase the document’s usability and clarity.

2. Framework Conditions, Organization, and Procedure for SPE Procedures  
The faculty management is responsible for the proper and planned implementation of evaluations of study programs and courses of study. On its behalf, the faculty QSE commissions or the faculty quality officers (Q-Beauftragte) and WiMa_Q, in coordination with the heads of the institutes and/or study programs, determine which study programs will be evaluated at which time. A medium- and long-term evaluation plan is drawn up following the principle of “rolling planning” and this is communicated faculty-wide. This creates transparency and planning security. Every study program and each course of study must be evaluated regularly, whereby a seven- to eight-year cycle should be aimed for in accordance with national and international standards (see Appendix 1). Interim evaluations (follow-ups) should also be planned, in which, about three years after completion of an SPE procedure or after the introduction of a new or revised curriculum, the effectiveness of the decided measures is reviewed (see Section 3.9).

1. **Introduction and Principles**

Quality assurance and quality development (QSE) have been considered a central element of all university areas at the University of Bern, not only since the implementation of the Higher Education Funding and Coordination Act (HFKG) in 2011. By establishing a comprehensive quality assurance system that covers all university performance areas, processes are systematized and appropriate QSE measures developed and implemented in all university areas. The goal is to continually further develop the quality of university achievements and to build a culture of quality.

The basis for revising existing or developing new QSE measures is the quality strategy of the University of Bern.

---

1.1 **Purpose of these Guidelines**

This document focuses on the university’s core tasks: teaching, research, continuing education, and services.1 The guidelines are addressed to the quality officers (Q-officers), scientific staff for quality (WiMaQ), and all persons responsible for the QSE of the core tasks in the faculties, strategic centers and centers with special assignments (hereinafter jointly referred to as "university centers") and in the central administration. In balancing subject-related, faculty, and university-wide interests, the approach follows the maxim “As differentiated as academically necessary, as uniform and comparable as possible”. This considers the specific needs and traditions of the various disciplines, while ensuring the required comparability.

Based on the university quality strategy, the QSE guidelines describe the principles of QSE at the University of Bern for the university’s core tasks and show how the various elements of QSE are operationalized (organization, communication, etc.). They primarily define basic conditions for the evaluation of teaching, research, continuing education, and services. They serve as a foundation for the faculties to formulate and further develop their own faculty guidelines for the QSE of the university's core tasks.2

1.2 **Foundations and Principles of these Guidelines**

The Federal Act on the Promotion of Universities and the Coordination in the Swiss Higher Education Sector (HFKG) defines the requirements for universities and, through institutional accreditation, ensures that universities develop quality assurance systems of their own and conduct quality assurance and development according to national and international criteria. The law creates the necessary basis for a competitive, permeable, and high-quality Swiss higher education area.

According to Art. 30, para. 1a of the HFKG, a prerequisite for institutional accreditation is that the university has a quality assurance system that ensures, among other things, the quality of the core tasks of teaching, research, and services. In the implementation of this requirement, the Standards and Guidelines for Quality Assurance in the European Higher Education Area (ESG) as well as the principles of the university evaluation regulations are taken into account. The guidelines for QSE of the university’s core tasks help to fulfill these requirements. Based on the legal requirements, they pursue the following principles:

- Alignment with the principle of excellent research and good teaching
- Ensuring a competitive scientific institution
- Ensuring and further developing a high level of quality awareness and thus establishing a university culture of quality
- Involvement and responsibility of all university members according to their areas of responsibility
- Consistency with the tasks, intentions and needs of the faculties, while taking into account the diversity of subject and faculty cultures.

1.3 **Steering and Reporting**

The central elements of quality assurance and quality development follow the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle (Figure 1). The PDCA cycle describes a continuous improvement process in which findings flow into quality planning, thus forming a control loop.

- Plan: The goals for the university core tasks are defined in the quality strategy, in the faculty strategies, or in the terms of reference for the university centers. The measures derived from these goals are documented in the university action plan as well as in the subsequent faculty action plans and the action plan of the strategic centers.
- Do: The responsible university units implement the measures established in the action plans for the university’s core tasks.
- Check: In order to check the extent to which the objectives have been achieved, meaningful, QSE-relevant indicators are defined.
- Act: In annual operational meetings, the university management checks whether and to what extent the faculties have achieved the objectives set in their action plans. The results of the review form the basis for updating the action plans. With new or adapted measures, changes in framework conditions, such as changes in faculty strategies, can be taken into account.

1.4 **QSE Organization**

QSE at the University of Bern is participative and is supported by all university members through delegated work in bodies (commissions, committees, etc.) or direct participation in the development, improvement and review of services (feedback, exchange, etc.).

The University Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission) advises the university management on all QSE matters. The faculties, the central administration, the Association of Lecturers (Oberer Mittelbau), the Association of Assistants (Unterer Mittelbau), and the students each have a Q-officer who is a member of the university-wide QSE Commission. Within the faculties, the Q-officers together with the WiMaQ are responsible for the implementation of the QSE. The faculties organize QSE according to faculty-specific needs. Ideally, there are faculty QSE bodies in which the departments are represented by faculty members (Q-person responsible [Department XY]) and positions (Q-person responsible [Position XY]). For specific topics, the Q-responsible persons of the university centers (Q-person responsible [Center XY]) can also be included.

The Accreditation Working Group (AKKRED), which operates independently of the QSE Commission, supports the university management, the central administration, the faculties, and the strategic centers in planning and reviewing the implementation of the university’s strategy. In cooperation with the central administration’s organizational units, AKKRED reviews and updates the university action plan annually according to the management’s guidelines. In addition, AKKRED supports the faculties in the annual updating of the faculty action plans and the strategic centers in the annual updating of the action plan for strategic centers.

2. **Guidelines for Evaluation of Teaching**

The development of teaching, and in particular of study programs or courses, is a central task of the faculties and is of primary concern to the students, the canton of Bern as the sponsoring body of the University of Bern, and the general public. Evaluations support the improvement and development of teaching. They serve the quality discussion and provide the information required for management. As recommended by the experts of the Quality Audits and institutional accreditation, external perspectives (e.g., the views of alumni or employers) should increasingly complement internal analyses. The nature, scope, and frequency of the evaluation are tailored to the purpose of the evaluation.

In addition, the assessment and selection of professors in structural and appointment procedures are of decisive importance for the QSE of teaching.

---

2.1 **Teaching Culture at the University of Bern: High Quality of Teaching and Learning**

The University of Bern understands high quality teaching to mean research-based and diverse instruction based on the scientific expertise of its teaching staff and meeting high didactical standards. Students at all qualifications levels should be guided through a partnership-based, dialog-oriented teaching and learning process towards creative, critical and interdisciplinary thinking, and prepared for responsible economic and societal roles.

Therefore, learning outcomes are formulated for the study programs, courses and classes. Teaching and examination methods are aligned with the learning outcomes. High-quality infrastructure and easy access to modern working media are ensured.

When designing teaching, attention is paid to the coherence of the study program, transitions between study levels, and bridges from school to university and from university to the professional world. Transparency regarding performance requirements and teaching processes helps students to organize their studies sensibly and helps early career researchers to prepare for an academic career as well as possible.

---

2.2 **Evaluation of Study Courses and Study Programs**

Evaluation critically reviews the quality of educational offerings (disciplinary, inter-, transdisciplinary Bachelor's and Master's programs and study programs, Graduate Schools, MAS and continuing education programs). The aim is to improve teaching by identifying strengths and weaknesses. The evaluation of program-, student-, and faculty-related measures and the results obtained make it possible to show students, supervisory authorities, and society that the University of Bern is dealing with further development of teaching and the promotion of early career researchers in an effective and efficient manner.

All study programs and study courses offered at the University of Bern are evaluated on a regular basis and with the involvement of external assessments. An evaluation cycle of ideally seven years, which can be deviated from in well-founded exceptions, should be aimed for. The faculties prepare binding evaluation plans accordingly.

For study programs and courses offered at the Medical Faculty (partly together with the Faculty of Science and the Vetsuisse Faculty) and evaluated and accredited under the MedBG by the Swiss Agency for Accreditation and Quality Assurance (AAQ) or other external institutions, specific rules apply; these are set out in the respective faculty QSE guidelines.

For study program evaluations, insofar as possible, data from the latest BFS graduate survey are taken into account. They must also cover the overall assessment of the performance assessments of the respective program.

Details are regulated in the university's guideline for the evaluation of study programs and courses at the University of Bern. The standardized processes for evaluating study programs / courses are regularly reviewed and further developed.

---

2.3 **Evaluation of Courses and Performance Assessments**

The evaluation of courses (consisting of interim evaluation and regulated final evaluation) and the evaluation of performance assessments should allow lecturers to consider the perspectives of students for the further development of their teaching. The goal is to design teaching-learning scenarios, including performance assessments, that facilitate the achievement of learning outcomes (improvement-oriented evaluation). In addition, the aggregated results of the final evaluation and the evaluation of the performance assessments serve to ensure the quality of teaching (accountability-oriented evaluation). To fulfill both purposes, a framework for course evaluation (including evaluation of performance assessments) was developed in cooperation with the faculties. Final evaluation and the evaluation of performance assessments using standardized instruments are carried out by the Course Evaluation Unit (Fachstelle LVE) of the Vice-Rectorate Quality and Sustainable Development in cooperation with the faculties. The use of an interim evaluation is available to lecturers as an additional tool and is strongly recommended. Faculties are free to use other evaluation instruments and procedures and to stipulate these in their QSE guidelines.

The following university-wide minimum requirements3 apply:

**Evaluation Criteria:**  
The evaluation of courses and performance assessments follows established criteria set out in a model. Threshold values for grading the evaluation results are established based on evidence. These criteria, threshold values, and classifications are defined in the framework for course evaluation.

**Cycle:**  
Each faculty prepares a binding evaluation plan that ensures all courses in every institute/department and 50% of all performance assessments are evaluated at least once every three years. The quality officers are responsible for compliance with the evaluation plan. For teaching staff in specific qualification positions (e.g., assistant professorships), evaluation is mandatory. Evaluation is also required for new lecturers’ courses and for courses that were rated inadequate in the previous semester. Additional evaluations for courses and performance assessments are possible and may be reported to the Course Evaluation Unit. Performance assessments are also evaluated collectively as part of the study program evaluation.

**Survey Tools**  
Standardized instruments, developed by the Course Evaluation Unit in cooperation with the faculties (see framework), are used for final and interim evaluations and for evaluating oral and written performance assessments. Faculties may use further instruments (e.g., focus group discussions, self-reports) for course evaluation. For other types of performance assessments, alternative data collection and evaluation methods are recommended. The standardized survey instruments and procedures are regularly reviewed and further developed.

**Handling of Results**  
Faculties take measures regarding courses rated as excellent or inadequate by students. These measures are defined in the framework for course evaluation and in the faculty QSE guidelines.

Further details about the evaluation of courses and performance assessments can be found in the framework for course evaluation.

3. **Guidelines for Evaluation of Research**

Science is based on integrity. The University of Bern grants researchers considerable freedom to foster the search for new knowledge and the expansion of the boundaries of understanding. But the freedom of researchers is not absolute; they must adhere to ethical and moral values, comply with laws and regulations, maintain scientific integrity, and handle funds, property, and data entrusted to them properly. The university management is committed to ensuring that research at Bern remains honest and fair. The regulations and directives on good scientific practice can be found on the University of Bern website and are part of employment contracts. The university also offers training in research integrity to raise awareness among students and staff.

The University of Bern is aware that the assessment and selection of professors in appointment procedures (Art. 63, UniV) is of decisive importance for the quality assurance and development of research. For this, the university is consistently committed to strategies for promoting early career researchers and to transparent and fair appointment procedures.

In addition to ensuring good scientific practice and high-quality appointment procedures, reflection on research achievements is central for the University of Bern. The evaluation of research achievements helps to identify strengths and weaknesses and offers a basis for recognizing opportunities for development and bringing about changes.

---

3.1 **Research Evaluation**

The University of Bern aims with its research evaluation to achieve transparency in the services provided, to develop decision-making aids for medium- and long-term planning, and to use the results for quality development in research. It is important that research evaluations are carried out within a carefully supported framework and, especially in terms of usefulness and resource allocation, follow the principles of good evaluation practice.

The University of Bern follows the recommendations of the Swiss Science Council (SWR) and those of swissuniversities in the design of its guidelines for research evaluation. The University of Bern foregoes the automatic allocation of funds based on evaluations. It respects disciplinary cultures and allows faculties the necessary leeway to adapt indicators and methods to specific needs in bottom-up processes, within university-wide requirements. The university sees this as key for successful reflection on research achievements, which is supported by the researchers and is aligned with the university’s objectives and its self-understanding as a learning organization.

---

3.2 **Procedures and Requirements in Research Evaluation**

The need for quantitative and qualitative information on research achievements determines research evaluation. Research activity includes both publication output and the overall research process. The subject of research evaluation includes faculties, departments, institutes, competence centers, research focal points, and other university units.

**3.2.1 Quantitative Procedures and Monitoring**  
The Vice-Rectorate Research and Innovation of the University of Bern4 systematically collects data on third-party funding, early career researcher support, knowledge transfer, and cooperation/networks6 in addition to publication output5 (and potential bibliometric findings considering the DORA Declaration), and provides it in the form of quantitative information for self-reflection and monitoring.

Quantitative indicators may be discussed in strategy meetings and included in the action plans of the faculties as indicators. They also flow into the structural reporting process and serve there, in addition to strategic considerations and external inputs, as a basis for discussion and decision-making. In the structural reporting process, at a minimum, information on bibliometrics, third-party funding, cooperations and early career researcher support is included to describe research activities. Additional indicators may be included at the request of the units and in consultation with those preparing the reports.

**3.2.2 Qualitative Procedures and Peer Reviews**  
In addition, the research performance of the units is regularly evaluated by external peers under clearly defined questions. The focus here is on positioning, clarifying the profile and development perspectives, assessment of current structures or assessment of research activities and results. The University of Bern considers it sensible to conduct external evaluations, for example when preparing for new appointments or structural reporting. External reviewers are also scheduled to serve as voting members of the committees in structural and appointment procedures. The following qualitative indicators should be considered in the evaluation of research work: research priorities of the unit; assessment of development goals and potential; publications (quality, originality, methodology, impact, etc.); third-party funding (sources and types); national/international, disciplinary/inter-/transdisciplinary, university/non-university cooperation/projects (networks, cooperations but also long-term projects); balance between research and teaching; involvement in university self-administration; recognition through honors, prizes, memberships, invitations, appointments, reviewing and consulting activities; conditions and achievements in early career researcher support (administrative burden on early career staff, opportunities for independent research/integration into research, opportunity for own teaching); knowledge transfer contributions with society and economy. An external review generally includes a self-assessment and an external assessment, both in the form of a brief report.

The unit/faculty being evaluated proposes a list of possible external reviewers to the Vice-Rectorate Research and Innovation. For an external review, care should be taken to ensure that, as a rule, at least three experts (for small fields, perhaps fewer as appropriate) answer and assess the questions and that the university's rules on conflicts of interest are observed. Those being evaluated have access to the reviewers' reports and can comment on their content. The faculty adopts the final report with findings, recommendations, and measures. The university management may review the procedure if necessary.

3. Purpose and Objective of the Peer Review Evaluation  
Based on the university’s QSE guidelines (Chapter 6), the organizational units (OE) of the Central Administration Area (ZB) at the University of Bern are regularly reviewed every eight years as part of an Informed Peer Review process. This involves a combination of self-evaluation (a self-reflection based on the quality requirements defined in the quality strategy) and external evaluation (review by external peers based on the self-evaluation report), forming a dialogue from internal and external perspectives. The peer review procedure is designed as a joint exchange in the sense of collegial consultation, focusing on mutual learning and the identification of strengths, weaknesses, and development potential.  
The thematic questions of the peer review procedure are oriented towards the performance description [of the OE] as well as the University Strategy 2030 and the evaluation criteria for the institutional reaccreditation of the Swiss Agency for Accreditation and Quality Assurance (AAQ). A key objective of the peer review is to identify strengths, weaknesses, and development potential [of the OE] with regard to the adequate fulfillment of the requirements of the university’s Strategy 2030 and its partial and functional strategies.

1. Introduction

Objectives, Forms, Responsibilities, and Procedures for External Evaluations

The Universität Bern stands for research that is recognized internationally, distinguished by high-quality teaching, targeted support of young talent, and consistent implementation of gender equality and sustainability goals.  
Quality assurance and development – referred to as QSE – contributes to continuously optimizing the quality of all university services, to establishing a unified culture of quality, and to working together on its further development. Furthermore, it promotes transparency and credibility with society and politics.

Quality at the university: What may seem self-evident actually requires collaboration at many different levels. In conversation, Silvia Schroer explains why it’s about more than just quantitative measurements, and where she still sees room for improvement.  
Interview: Vanessa Naef, Interdisciplinary Gender Studies Center IZFG, Universität Bern  
What does "quality" mean in the university context, and specifically for Universität Bern?  
I would say quite clearly: Quality at a university is no different than in a craft. When we hire a craftsperson, we expect them to be well-trained and to do the job competently. In the same way, everything done at the university must meet very high standards, and its fulfillment must be verifiable. Not only do students, researchers, and staff have this right, but so do the public and policymakers. After all, the university is not only subject to the law, but is also largely funded by taxpayer money.  
"Everything done at the university must meet high standards."

12  
This means that society also has the right to the highest possible quality. Our aim is to fulfill this right, and that is why we are constantly working on quality at all levels of the extremely complex university system.  
This system, with its variety of competences, is highlighted from the outset in the university’s quality strategy 2018–2021: “Quality assurance and development is a complex system and a culture that permeates the entire university.” Where does the complexity of this system lie, and what challenges and tasks arise for the university?  
The guiding principle stresses that quality cannot be created at the push of a button, but requires lengthy processes. Building a culture of quality across the university requires a lot of cooperation. It is important to involve everyone in building this culture. It’s not about measuring all the time, but rather about practicing careful observation. Exams and reviews are not an end in themselves. The results should feed back into the starting point, leading to adaptations or changes. We call this closing the control loop. Simply put: It’s about improvement—which of course does not mean that previous things were bad. Everything that we review through quality assurance should ultimately result in progress.  
With institutional accreditation, the university proves that it has an outstanding quality assurance system.  
Quality is like a virtue.

13  
The biggest challenge, in my view, is planning for time in a meaningful way. We have to be aware that we can’t change or develop everything at once—we can try, but it requires prioritization and staging of goals. The upcoming institutional accreditation in Spring 2021 is currently a major task. The external institutions assisting the university in this review are not there to assess, for instance, whether teaching in a particular field is good. Instead, they focus on whether we have a good system to find out if teaching is good – thus, the focus is on the gearwork of quality-oriented development. We must convincingly demonstrate that we have a good quality assurance system.  
What does the culture of quality at Universität Bern look like? Can you give a few successful examples?  
One well-known method is the – now electronic – questionnaires for course evaluation. This feedback tool for various aspects of teaching is very important for both students and instructors. The key is what happens when feedback is unsatisfactory. Here, a regulated procedure is necessary. What happens with repeated poor ratings? Who at an institute or faculty is responsible for discussing this with the affected instructors? We allow plenty of freedom as to how–the procedure isn’t prescribed by the faculty–but we ensure that something actually happens; otherwise, the evaluation and questionnaires would be pointless.  
"At a university, quality is no different than in craft." Silvia Schroer  
Quality assurance is always considered from the start, not only "retrofitted" later.

14  
A lesser-known example of quality assurance is waste disposal, which is a daily part of university life. The university management recently adopted revised disposal guidelines. That may sound trivial—just about collecting the “rubbish”. But this also concerns hazardous waste, radioactive and biomedical waste, which affects some faculties significantly. Strict regulations, in keeping with the law, are needed, since the public and employees have a right to health and safety; employees are obliged to follow regulations and must be well informed. Responsibility for some sensitive processes lies with the risk management team, but again, all students and staff share the responsibility.  
What are the key developments currently shaping teaching, research, continuing education, and services?  
A lot is happening in teaching, especially in connection with digitalization. The pace of these changes gives us great challenges. The university management has responded with a digitalization strategy. Some objectives have already been implemented. From the outset, care was taken to ensure sustainability and quality assurance—even with the rapid pace of changes, these were not only considered afterwards.
We also try to make accounting for diversity and sustainability a consistent theme—not only in teaching, but also in research, continuing education, and services.  
For example, how do appointment processes work when hiring staff? What must be done to permanently increase the proportion of female professors across the university? The university sets requirements for the process itself. Structural and appointment committees are always accompanied by a representative from the Gender Equality office. For example, they ensure applications are not only rated by number of publications, as Universität Bern has signed the DORA declaration. This means that, when hiring, a selection of publications should be assessed for quality rather than quantity alone. There are also recommendations and guidelines at the university for sustainability.  
The climate-friendly university is becoming an ever-greater topic. How can the total amount of air travel and CO2 emissions be reduced?  
"We always try to thread gender equality and sustainability as a common thread." Silvia Schroer  
The university’s digitalization strategy was developed in harmony with sustainability and quality assurance.

The system of QSE is complex. Therefore, a finely tuned organization in which roles and responsibilities are clearly defined is needed.  
QSE at the university is designed to be participatory and is supported by all members—whether through involvement in committees as part of academic self-governance, or through direct participation in the development, improvement, and review of services. The Quality Assurance and Development Commission (QSE Commission) advises university management on all QSE matters. The faculties, central administration, mid-level staff, and students each send a quality representative to the QSE Commission. In the faculties, faculty QSE committees—representing subject areas and stakeholder groups—fulfill this task for the faculty.  
These QSE specialist committees act as information interfaces between the faculties and their disciplinary subdivisions and ensure that communication and control loops are always closed. ■

Initial Situation and Objectives

The Communication Concept for University Quality Assurance and Development (QSE) – abbreviated as Communication Concept QSE – aims to comprehensively and adequately inform all university members about measures, procedures, results, the significance and meaningfulness of the university QSE and to involve them in its development. Key documents and regulations concerning QSE are publicly available and known to all staff and students.

Communication on QSE is an essential part of the university’s quality culture. It supports the internal university discussion culture through direct and active engagement with specific groups via UniBE communication channels and through the daily work of the QSE department in Q-committees and channels for the Q-community. Information is provided regularly about the goals, procedures, results, and best practices in QSE, exchange and networking are promoted, and the meaning, purpose, and added value of quality development for students and staff are made visible.

The communication concept is based on the following foundations:
•	Strategy 2030 of the University of Bern
•	Quality Strategy 2022
•	Procedural Report Institutional Accreditation UniBE
•	Guide for Institutional Accreditation

The implementation of the QSE communication concept takes place in cooperation with the vice-rectorates, faculties, university centers, as well as the Department for Communication and Marketing (AKM).

For specific QSE processes and projects such as institutional accreditation, the evaluation and accreditation of degree programs or the certification of services, further communication measures may be defined, which are based on the quality strategy and this communication concept.

Target Groups

QSE communication is aimed at students, professors, management personnel, and all other QSE responsible persons in the faculties, university centers, and the central administration, as well as at the public.

a. Students

The education of students, and thus teaching, is a core university task, making students the most important target group for communication. They witness the University of Bern’s commitment to teaching and are its reference group. Primarily, students are interested in the quality of teaching and campus life (including libraries, IT infrastructure, cafeterias, student workspaces, university sports, job and housing exchange).
As students and alumni, they are ambassadors for the university: They convey the university’s image as a place of education, as a teaching university, and of excellent teaching—even after graduation as alumni and as future employees and employers. Through the knowledge and competencies they acquire, they shape culture, society, the economy, politics, and science.

Communication Concept QSE Page 3

b. Professors and Management Personnel

Professors, members of management bodies, and leadership personnel are important representatives of the university. They are seen as high achievers, experts, teachers, and researchers in the focus of students, society, and politics. Thus, they have a role model function.

c. Commission for Quality Assurance and Development, Q-Officers, Q-Responsible Persons, Scientific Quality Staff, Members of AKKRED, and all other employees with QSE tasks

In their functions within the university, they embody the organizational and thus the quality culture and are responsible for the content and implementation of QSE in the faculties, departments, institutes, university centers, and organizational units of the central administration. With conceptual and operational responsibility for QSE measures, this target group works at the interface between overall university goals and partial interests.

d. Other University Members, Public, Politics and Media, Employers

For the perception of the overall university quality culture, it is essential to specifically address the public about the strategies, instruments, and procedures for teaching, research, and program evaluation and their results, as well as about the accreditation process or other quality-related topics. The public has an interest and the right to know what achievements the university provides in research and teaching and as an employer.

According to Strategy 2030, quality assurance and development is one of seven functional strategies that are of central importance for the functionality of the university. In this context, the University of Bern utilizes the system of quality assurance and development to continuously improve its performance. The faculties formulate their contribution to the realization of the functional strategies as well as their specific goals through faculty strategies. Core components of the quality management system are the quality strategy and action plans at both the university and faculty levels for their implementation.

The Quality Strategy of the University of Bern 2022 defines the framework for quality assurance and development (QSE) measures and demonstrates how the quality management system functions. This ensures the sustainable further development of the quality management system as well as the establishment of values and a shared understanding of the university’s quality culture. The university QSE is based on a lived quality culture, efficient governance, and measures and processes for the implementation of QSE.

The QSE at the University of Bern is designed as a participatory system that integrates into existing management systems. The QSE is conceived as an open, dynamic system that is continuously developed in terms of content. This allows the University of Bern, through guided self-reflection—incorporating external expertise if necessary—a flexible adaptation to external circumstances and developments. The quality assurance system guarantees the implementation of the university strategy and the development of the University of Bern.

All areas of the University of Bern are regularly evaluated. This ensures the ongoing review of the quality of university services and the development of a shared quality culture that simultaneously respects the individual characteristics of the different teaching and research cultures of the faculties and their autonomy. The guiding principle here is: “As differentiated (professionally) as necessary, as uniform (and comparable) as possible.”

Quality Culture  
Quality culture consists of structural-formal and organizational-psychological elements (Sonntag and Sattler 2017). Structural-formal elements are found at the normative, strategic, and operational levels of quality assurance. These include, in particular, regulations, directives, as well as guidelines and specific evaluation tools for teaching, research, and the services of the central QSE unit. Key organizational-psychological elements for the development of a shared quality culture include leadership understanding, participation, communication, commitment, engagement, and sense of responsibility.

The QSE at the University of Bern is highly participatory. All university members contribute through their participation in bodies such as commissions and committees, or steer it through their involvement in the development, improvement, and review of work (feedback, exchange, etc.). Care is taken to keep the processes streamlined to reduce the burden on those responsible at faculty level and, moreover, to ensure a high level of participation among university members in the process in the long term.

The QSE System  
To formulate, achieve, and maintain the highest quality goals, the University of Bern is guided by the quality cycle (Deming cycle, PDCA-cycle), as shown in Figure 1. The relevant goals are primarily found in Strategy 2030, the performance mandate of the Government Council to the University of Bern for the years 2022–2025, the institutional accreditation process report, and the functional strategies (Appendix 1). Quality control is the part of the quality assurance system that defines and implements measures to achieve the quality goals. The measures for implementing the quality goals are recorded in the University Action Plan and the Faculty Action Plans. Using the Faculty Action Plans and the annual strategy discussions with the faculties and centers, it is checked whether the set goals have been achieved. The information obtained from analyses and evaluations flows back into planning, thus closing the control loop.

This leads to process optimizations and to measures that improve the structure. Quality control also includes quality documentation. This involves transparent documentation of efforts to improve quality and of the measures and adjustments to processes. This provides evidence, for example through accreditation, that the quality requirements are being met.

The University Action Plan lists all measures of the university QSE and is thus the most comprehensive document of measures for the QSE. Together, the University Action Plan and the Faculty Action Plans make all university-level and faculty- and center-specific quality assurance and development measures visible and serve as strategic controlling of the implementation of Strategy 2030 and the faculty strategies. The action plans are structured according to the university’s four pillars—Tasks, Personnel, Cross-cutting Issues, Resources—as well as the category Governance.

Measures from the University Action Plan that fall within the responsibility of the faculties or centers are listed in the Faculty Action Plans. The measures from the University Action Plan provide the framework for minimum requirements in implementation. The Faculty Action Plans define how the measures falling within the faculties’ responsibility are implemented at the faculty and center level. To implement the faculty strategies, the Faculty Action Plans are updated based on the objectives of the faculty strategies by means of additional measures.

With the Quality Strategy 2022, the University Action Plan, and the Faculty Action Plans, the University of Bern has the necessary instruments at its disposal to develop the desired unified quality culture and to set priorities in implementation. The jointly defined values and objectives promote the quality awareness of university members and shared values with regard to quality. All those who research, teach, study, and work at the University of Bern, as well as all organizational units and committees, share responsibility for the quality of university services.

Holistic Approach of the University QSE System  
Based on Strategy 2030, the quality standards for institutional accreditation (according to Art. 22, para. 1 Accreditation Ordinance HFKG, Appendix 2), and in line with the self-understanding of the University of Bern, a model was developed that includes all university areas in the QSE. As such, the model includes the areas specified in the accreditation guidelines that must necessarily be covered by the quality assurance system. The respective areas are assigned to the four pillars: Tasks, Personnel, Cross-cutting Issues, or Resources, with Governance acting as the overarching framework.

The quality assurance system of the University of Bern follows a comprehensive approach that permanently guarantees quality as a system goal. The quality assurance system sufficiently takes into account the specificities of a university (especially academic freedom). Measures for QSE include, on the one hand, the university’s core activities—teaching, research, service, and continuing education—as well as all university members from professors to students (see Figure 2); on the other hand, the cross-cutting themes of gender equality, sustainable development, internationalization, communication, and digitalization, as well as resources—personnel recruitment and development, finance, infrastructure, administration and operations, and risk management.

1. Introduction
1.1. About This Document
The purpose of this document is to give an overview of the measures for quality assurance and development (QSE) at the course level at the University of Bern. The document is primarily aimed at quality officers (Q-officers), academic staff for quality (WiMaQ), staff in institutes and departments, as well as at faculty committees responsible for QSE on behalf of the faculty leadership. Furthermore, this document is directed at all persons and groups interested in the quality of teaching (instructors, students).
In 2023, the present framework concept was updated in connection with changes in the evaluation process made in 2022–2023 (including the discontinuation of paper surveys, regulations regarding access to data and reports).

1.2. Revision Project August 2018–February 2020 and Update of the Framework Concept 2023
This document is the result of the project "Revision of Course Evaluations," conducted from August 2018 to spring 2020 by the Unit for Course Evaluation (Fachstelle LVE), which was then part of the ICT-based Teaching and Research Support Unit (iLUB). A goal of the project was to develop a university-wide framework concept for course evaluations that is useful and practical for the faculties. As recommended by the panel of experts in the context of the Quality Audit 2013/2014 (OAQ, 2014), the previously pronounced differentiation of QSE measures should not be continued but rather simplified and refocused. As far as possible, teaching should be assessed and managed in uniform, comparable, practical, and clearly communicable processes. The application and adherence to the evaluation guidelines should be bindingly required, systematically fed back to faculty leadership, and be associated with consequences. Additionally, according to the panel of experts, it should be checked to what extent a course actually imparts the skills and competencies that are tested in the examinations. Finally, binding processes for the evaluation of examination procedures should be introduced.
After the pilot phase in 2019–2020, the framework concept was introduced in the course of 2020.
As planned, the instruments were reviewed again after three years (2023) in terms of quality assurance and were adapted in collaboration with the faculties, which ultimately led to the update of this framework concept.

1.3. Embedding Course Evaluation in QSE
Successful institutional accreditation of the university requires that the institution "[...] has a quality assurance system that ensures that teaching, research, and services are of high quality and that its staff are appropriately qualified." (HFKG, 2011, Article 30).
Evaluation at the course level is a building block in the QSE guidelines for the core university tasks of teaching, research, continuing education, and services at the University of Bern:

4
"The evaluation of courses (consisting of midterm evaluation and regulated final evaluation) and the evaluation of examinations should allow instructors to incorporate students' perspectives for the further development of their teaching. (…) Furthermore, the aggregated results of the final evaluation and the evaluation of examinations serve to ensure the quality of teaching (accountability-oriented evaluation)." (Universität Bern, 2023)
Course evaluation, as a central QSE instrument for teaching, is also anchored in the university's quality strategy (Universität Bern, 2022).

2. Standards of Course Evaluation
2.1. Purpose
The results of the final evaluation, the evaluation of examinations, and especially the midterm evaluation serve for improvement, as described in the university’s QSE guidelines (improvement-oriented evaluation). The goal is to identify strengths and weaknesses in teaching at the course level to generate ideas for modification (cf. Balzer & Beywl, 2018). The results of standardized course evaluation (primarily the final evaluation and evaluation of examinations) also serve for quality assurance at institute, faculty, and university level (accountability-oriented evaluation).

2.2. Process
Since the revision, the evaluation is distributed across three measurement points. (1) First, instructors have access to a standardized questionnaire for the midterm evaluation in the middle of the semester1, which is primarily aimed at formative evaluation of the course. (2) For the final evaluation, the established and effective processes of the existing standard evaluation are used. Refocusing compared to the previous evaluation process has been achieved by now using the same shortened and adapted core questionnaire for most faculties and all types of courses in the final evaluation. Due to binding evaluation plans, each faculty guarantees that all courses are evaluated every three years. (3) Existing evaluation processes are additionally complemented by the evaluation of examinations.
The quality-relevant dimensions for the three evaluation types (midterm evaluation, final evaluation, and evaluation of examinations) are aligned according to the model of teaching quality (see Figure 5 in the Appendix).

Any instructor may also voluntarily register for evaluation (midterm evaluation, final evaluation, and/or evaluation of examinations). Instructors and faculties are also free to use other data collection instruments or evaluation methods, such as peer observations, focus group discussions, etc., as a complement. They may also use offerings from university didactics & teaching development2.
Since autumn semester 2022, all course evaluations at the University of Bern have been conducted online. Paper surveys are no longer used3.

Unlike other faculties, the Faculty of Medicine has its own unit for the evaluation of teaching. The evaluation concepts of the various degree programs are regularly reviewed and, if necessary, optimized in cooperation with the Dean of Studies Office. Midterm and final evaluations take place exclusively online, with different questionnaires used depending on the degree program, level, and format (lecture vs. practical training). A systematic evaluation of the quality of examinations is currently being developed by the "Examinations" working group in the Faculty of Medicine. The examination committees may already request an evaluation if necessary.

2.2.1. Midterm Evaluation
The essentially voluntary midterm evaluation of a course is carried out by the Unit for Course Evaluation using a standardized questionnaire, after appropriate registration. The questionnaire includes both quantitative and qualitative questions. The midterm evaluation allows instructors to gather opinions and suggestions from students early in the course to improve the course already during the semester. To increase the response rate, it is recommended to conduct the midterm evaluation in plenary during the course.
The statistical analysis is performed by the evaluation system evasys. Free-text responses are included in the report. The report automatically generated by evasys from the midterm evaluation is sent to the instructors of the course unless otherwise agreed and is not used for organizational quality assurance. It is recommended that instructors communicate the results of the midterm evaluation to students during the course and discuss them with them. Additionally, it is desirable that the instructors comment on the evaluation results so that students learn how the results are used.
Midterm evaluation is recommended to all instructors as a beneficial supplement. However, instructors are also free to use other methods for collecting midterm feedback4.
Midterm evaluation may be required in the case of "inadequate" results from a final evaluation (see section 2.3).

2.2.2. Final Evaluation
The final evaluation serves, on the one hand, for accountability5 and allows for monitoring the development of teaching quality at the University of Bern. On the other hand, the final evaluation contains improvement-oriented elements. It serves to provide instructors with data-based indications in which areas a course needs improvement. When the final evaluation follows a midterm evaluation, it shows to what extent actions taken after the midterm evaluation have been successfully implemented.
The final evaluation is conducted using standardized questionnaires managed by the Unit for Course Evaluation and takes place near the end of the semester or the course. It is recommended that the process is carried out at the latest during the second to last class session, so the results can still be discussed with the students. To increase the response rate, it is recommended to conduct the final evaluation in plenary during the course. Individual additional modules6 may be added to the core questionnaire, which is the same for all course types, upon request.
Courses to be evaluated are selected in the faculties according to the QSE guidelines and recorded in a binding plan. In this way, it is guaranteed that all courses of all institutes/departments, in both the spring and autumn semester, are evaluated at least once every three years. For new instructors and those in certain qualification positions (e.g., assistant professorships), evaluation is mandatory (see QSE guidelines of Universität Bern, 2023). The selection process is managed by the Q-officers in the faculties.
The automated analysis of the final evaluation, including the listing of free-text answers (the minimum number of responses is five7), is sent to the instructors responsible for the respective course (if not otherwise indicated, according to KSL8). Other staff and committees with QSE duties can request detailed reports from the Unit for Course Evaluation. The principle of purpose and proportionality is decisive9. The anonymity of respondents and the protection of collected data is ensured10.
Instructors are required to inform students of the results of the final evaluation during the course and discuss them with them. Using the final evaluation report, instructors can also see into which success category their course falls (the categorization ranges from ‘inadequate’ to ‘outstanding’). The thresholds for this categorization were determined in collaboration with the faculties and an external expert. The determining indicators for the categorization are "learning progress" and "satisfaction" in the core questionnaire for the final evaluation. Categorization as "outstanding" is linked to the project "Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching" (ALL)11. Categorization as "inadequate" is linked to measures (see section 2.3).
After all final evaluations in a given semester are completed, the faculty quality officers (unless otherwise agreed, these are the WiMaQ) receive an aggregated data analysis for further processing and possible forwarding to institutes/departments.

2.2.3. Evaluation of Examinations
Examinations are an important part of teaching as they influence the learning behavior of students and often the teaching design of instructors as well (Metzger & Nüesch, 2004; Schindler, 2015; Frölich-Steffen, 2019).
The evaluation of examinations (ELK) provides data-based indications to instructors and faculty examination committees about what can be improved in the examinations. Furthermore, the ELK serves purposes of accountability12. The ELK is conducted by the Unit for Course Evaluation using standardized questionnaires (further details on implementation, see section 2.2.3.2).
Faculties guarantee in their faculty QSE guidelines that 50% of all examinations are evaluated regularly (at least every 3 years).
To ensure regular review of examinations, all faculties are therefore encouraged to establish an examination committee or to delegate the tasks of the examination committee to an existing committee (e.g., the faculty QSE commission).

2.2.3.1.
Faculty Examination Committees
The faculty examination committee, or the committees or offices carrying out these tasks, should ensure that when designing13 and planning evaluations, the opinions of all groups (professors, mid-level academic staff, students) are taken into account as far as possible. It is possible to include a representative from the area of university didactics & teaching development or from the Unit for Course Evaluation. The faculty examination committee or the committees or offices carrying out these tasks will, at the prescribed intervals14, sample a pre-defined selection of examinations determined by the faculty. After analyzing the evaluation results, they develop recommendations for improving the examinations and report to the faculty QSE commission. To do this, the committee or appropriate body can use the results of examination evaluation surveys, analyze average grades, request self-evaluation reports from the responsible instructors/exam coordinators, or apply other own data collection and evaluation methods tailored to the faculty’s exam formats. Possible topics suitable for the evaluation of examinations include the appropriateness of the exam format, fairness, the coherence between the formulated learning outcomes, the teaching methods used and the exam content, the preparation of students for the exam (regular vs. last-minute studying), difficulty level of the exam, and students’ self-assessed learning success.

2.2.3.2.
Implementation of the Evaluation of Examinations
The standard questionnaire can primarily be used to evaluate written and oral exams that are directly connected to a course.
Exam formats that are not written or oral examinations (e.g., essays, presentations, practical exams, or those composed of several assessments) can also be evaluated using the standard questionnaire if needed and upon request.
When registering examinations for evaluation with the standard questionnaire, care must be taken to ensure that only those examinations are registered for which a sufficient response rate can be expected. (Reports are generated with at least n=5 valid responses. If there are too few responses, no reports are generated.)
For examinations, especially those with few participants, formats that are not written or oral exams, or which are less suitable for evaluation with the standard questionnaire, faculties may use alternative data collection and evaluation methods (e.g., focus group discussions, evaluations in the context of study program evaluations, review of exam concepts by university didactics, etc.)15.
ELK using the standard questionnaire should be conducted after the exam16 and—if possible—before the grades are published.
Surveys on written exams remain open for four days—and for oral exams for 24 hours—unless otherwise agreed. Thus, grades for written exams should be entered no earlier than four days after the exam17, and for oral exams no earlier than after 24 hours.
After the entry of grades is confirmed to the Unit for Course Evaluation18, the report is validated by the unit and sent to the instructors responsible for the respective examination (if not otherwise indicated, according to KSL). If no notification is provided, the Unit for Course Evaluation sends the reports to the instructors after a deadline agreed with the faculty quality officers (WiMaQ) (e.g., eight weeks after the last ELK).
Other staff and committees with QSE duties can request detailed reports from the Unit for Course Evaluation. The principle of purpose and proportionality is decisive19.
The anonymity of respondents and the protection of collected data is ensured. If possible, the results of the ELK should be communicated to students via the respective ILIAS course or other channels related to the course.
After all ELK in a given semester and grades have been released, the faculty quality officers (WiMaQ) receive an aggregated analysis of the valid evaluations in the form of mean value tables. The minimum response quota of n=5 also applies here.
Instructors also have access to various university didactics tools, such as the Teaching Tool Assessment or the Assessment Toolbox20, to review and improve the quality of their own exams. Instructors can also contact the area of university didactics & teaching development for individual consultation or use the available training on examinations21 at any time.

2.3. Closing the Quality Loops
A course that is rated as "inadequate" in the final evaluation must, in consultation with the Q-officer, be registered again for evaluation during its next offering22. In addition to the final evaluation, a midterm evaluation must also be performed during the repeated offering.
If, after this, the repeated final evaluation again results (for the second time) in only the "inadequate" category, the instructor is asked by the Q-officer to submit a short written self-report23 commenting on the three evaluations and their results and to reflect on their own teaching. Among other things, the instructor should explain a) to what extent framework conditions or other factors might have influenced the results and b) what measures have been or will be taken by the instructor to improve teaching. Which persons the self-report must be sent to is determined in the faculty QSE guidelines.
If on renewed final evaluation (second time) only "inadequate" is achieved, the Q-officer of the respective faculty shall also arrange a meeting with the affected instructor24, to jointly identify the reasons and search for suitable measures to improve the situation. The Unit for Course Evaluation may be involved for interpreting the result as well as in the meeting. The aim of the meeting is to understand the results of the course evaluation in context, to extract data-based indications from the results, and to also identify which aspects need improvement. If, after these measures, the following evaluation is again (third time) classified as "inadequate," the instructor is required to attend a course from the wide range offered by university didactics or to seek individual advice25.

In order to also close the quality loop for the evaluation of examinations, the examination committees or agencies carrying out these tasks must explain in their report to the faculty, among other things, which conclusions can be drawn from the evaluation results and which measures have been or will be taken. Likewise, the results of the ELK should be incorporated into the evaluations of study programs/degrees.
The faculties’ Q-officers are responsible for ensuring that the measures described in this concept are implemented. These include the implementation of the final evaluation and the evaluation of examinations, the submission of self-reports, the possible conducting of discussions, further support measures for quality development, and reporting by examination committees.
The aim of these quality loops is to ensure and further develop the quality of teaching. In this, the role of instructors must be considered, as well as the influence of the framework conditions and other factors. In addition, a culture of evaluation should be promoted in which instructors openly and gladly discuss the further development of their teaching. A positive evaluation culture is understood to mean that the evaluation of teaching has a high priority within the organization and that evaluation is a proven means for instructors to improve their own teaching quality. Open, appreciative communication about the results of course and exam evaluations supports a positive evaluation culture and thus contributes to a university-wide quality culture.

Quality assurance and development (QSE) is designed to be participatory and is supported by all members of the university, through participation in committees or direct involvement in the development, improvement, and evaluation of services (feedback, exchange, etc.).

With this award, the university recognizes and honors the commitment of its instructors to outstanding teaching.

ALL helps to improve the quality of teaching, promote appreciation for excellent teaching, and advance the development of good teaching at the University of Bern as a whole. In addition, it provides academic mid-level staff with the opportunity to distinguish themselves in teaching.

The criteria, the selection process, and the form of the award are set out in the “Concept for the Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching” (accessible internally).

Objective

In today's world, where education and knowledge are of crucial importance, instructors play a central role. They not only impart knowledge, but also serve as mentors who inspire, encourage, and support students. Outstanding courses can have a lasting impact on students' lives and careers. In recognition of this importance, the University of Bern honors exceptionally well-evaluated courses and showcases them as examples. 
Moreover, this gives mid-level academic staff the opportunity to distinguish themselves in teaching and to present an official confirmation of positively evaluated courses when applying for positions.

Procedure
The faculties or institutes determine which courses will be evaluated in the respective semester (see framework concept). (For the time being, evaluations from the Faculty of Medicine are not considered, as they use a different evaluation format.)
The Office of Course Evaluation (Dept. QSE, Vice-Rectorate for Quality and Sustainable Development) evaluates the selected courses.
After conducting and analyzing all evaluations, the Office of Course Evaluation nominates, around mid-March for HS and mid-September for FS, those instructors whose courses meet the established criteria.
The faculties review the nominations and inform the Office of Course Evaluation about the awarded courses/instructors. 
The faculties access the award template via the university's internal area, complete it with the relevant information, and print the diploma. The diploma is signed by the dean and handed over to the respective individuals.
The Office of Course Evaluation publishes the awarded courses/instructors on the website.

Criteria
To be nominated for ALL, a course must achieve an “outstanding” overall result in the final evaluation. The determining factors for classification as “outstanding” are the results in the categories “learning progress” and “satisfaction”.

Since HS22, the threshold for the “outstanding” level is ≥ 5.7. You can find more information here

In addition to this university-wide criterion, faculties may define individual criteria for the awarding of ALL:

Additional ALL Criteria by Faculty

Faculty of Humanities (Phil-Hist) has had the following additional criteria since HS22. In addition to an overall score of at least 5.7, the courses must meet the following minimum scores for the two indicators, learning progress and satisfaction:
Courses with 1–10 survey participants: at least 5.65
Courses with 11–20 survey participants: at least 5.55
Courses with 21–30 survey participants: at least 5.45
Courses with 31 or more survey participants: at least 5.35
For courses with fewer than 15 evaluation forms, the institute is asked if the course did not actually have significantly more participants. For 6 or fewer evaluation forms, the institute is asked if granting the award is appropriate. Courses with less participation from the instructor (e.g. research colloquia, lecture series) are excluded from ALL.

Faculty of Law (RW) has had no additional criteria since HS22

1.
Introduction
1.1
Fundamentals and Principles
Based on national legislation, the University of Bern is obligated to ensure and document quality assurance and development (QSE) in its core functions of teaching, research, and continuing education.1
On this basis, the interdisciplinary and/or interfaculty centers at the University of Bern with a strategic orientation or special mandate are each subjected to a self-evaluation process in preparation for new performance mandates. Evaluations2 are an integral part of the QSE system at the University of Bern.
The planning, design, implementation, and analysis of evaluations are the responsibility of the centers in coordination with the faculties organizationally assigned to them under their performance mandates, and they orient themselves to the university and faculty standards and guidelines3.
The self-evaluations of the centers are primarily a QSE tool that enables systematic reflection on the respective profile and the quality of the research and, where applicable, other services at the level of the evaluating units from different perspectives. In these, quantitative and qualitative data and information regarding the substantive concepts and their up-to-dateness and strategic relevance, as well as structures and organization, are documented and processed. Thus, the evaluations serve the goal of optimal service delivery and profile sharpening of the centers or the university institutes involved in them, by helping to further develop and, if necessary, correct content, methods, and framework conditions.
Furthermore, the evaluations are important for the development and positioning of the university as a scientific and research institution as well as for the faculties, departments, and institutes. In the strategy meetings of the university leadership (UL) with the centers and faculties or in structural report procedures, evaluation results may play a role as a planning element.
1.2
This Document
To support those responsible for QSE in the centers and the organizationally assigned faculties, the present recommendations and guidance for conducting a self-evaluation are provided. This document has been developed based on the evaluation procedures conducted in the centers and the experiences gained therein, and replaces the guide for the self-evaluation of competence centers at the University of Bern approved in March 2013.
The revised guide describes the framework conditions and objectives of evaluation procedures for centers, their sequence, and responsibilities in the evaluation process. The guidance and recommendations are meant as an orientation and—except for elements marked as "mandatory"—are not binding. Nevertheless, they should be taken into account as far as possible to ensure that the evaluation procedures can be carried out successfully.

2.
Framework Conditions and Organization of Self-Evaluations
Each center management is responsible for the proper implementation of an evaluation. The timing for an evaluation results from the performance period specified in the respective performance mandate. The clearly defined, cyclically designed performance periods ensure transparency and planning reliability regarding the resources to be made available for each evaluation procedure.

A fully conducted and positively assessed self-evaluation by the university leadership is the basis and prerequisite for the granting of a new performance mandate and the associated allocation of financial resources.
The evaluation procedures are basically participatory. Thus, it is possible, based on the analyses carried out in the course of the evaluation, to discuss and further develop research and other activities and infrastructural conditions in the light of the requirements formulated in the respective performance mandates as well as scientific, university, and societal demands with all stakeholder groups and external support.
From these premises, the following, partly overlapping steps normally occur in an evaluation procedure:
1. Preparation and planning
2. Formation of the evaluation committee incl. external experts
3. Development of an evaluation concept and timeline
4. Data collection and analysis in accordance with the evaluation concept
5. Preparation and approval of the final report and action plan
6. Report analysis and development of the new performance mandate
7. Follow-up reporting
8. Communication

3.
The Self-Evaluation Procedure
3.1
Preparation and Planning
As a rule, the scientific associate (WiMa) in the Division for Quality Assurance and Development (QSE) in the Vice-Rectorate (VR) Quality initiates preparations for the evaluation procedure about 16 months before the end of the performance period defined in the current performance mandate. Afterward, the center management or the person responsible for quality for the center (Q-responsible) clarifies the key procedural issues with the QSE Division (see checklist in Appendix 1).
Furthermore, the center management or the Q-responsible person prepares a timeline and a draft of an evaluation concept (see section 3.3) and seeks suitable members for the evaluation committee (see section 3.2). They also clarify whether and to what extent financial resources are needed for the evaluation.
The center management determines how the members of the relevant center will be informed about the upcoming evaluation (see section 3.8).

3.2
Evaluation Committee incl. External Experts
The term "evaluation committee" is generic. The centers are free to designate the body convened for the evaluation as they wish.
In order to best achieve the requirements and objectives of the centers' self-evaluations, it is desirable to include a variety of perspectives on the items to be evaluated. All status groups involved in a center should, therefore, be represented in an evaluation committee.
After its establishment, the committee decides on the evaluation concept (see section 3.3) and the associated timeline (see section 3.3.4). It is responsible for carrying out the evaluation, meets as required during the process, and delegates tasks to individual members or other persons.
The evaluation committee is usually composed of the following members:
• Q-responsible of the respective center
• Representative of the center management
• Representative of mid-level academic staff
• Representative of administrative and/or technical personnel
• possibly external person(s) (see below)
• possibly representative of teaching staff
• possibly representative of students

The scientific associate in the QSE Division responsible for center evaluations accompanies the processes in an advisory and supportive capacity. The faculty Q-officers and WiMa_Q can be consulted for specific questions that are only to be answered internally within the faculty.

The university has made the inclusion of external assessment and expertise in the evaluation of the quality of teaching, research, and the promotion of young academics mandatory. The aim of external assessment is to independently review and, if necessary, complement the internal perspective, and to help interpret the available data. The external perspective4 is ensured either by including one or more peer persons in the evaluation committee or by commissioning one or more external reviews. The decision depends on the questions to the external experts. In all cases, early consideration and planning of this aspect is strongly recommended.

3.3
Evaluation Concept and Timeline
With the evaluation concept, the evaluation committee lays important groundwork for the upcoming evaluation procedure. It establishes the strategic goals and guiding questions, individual steps, deadlines, and responsibilities in a binding manner.
Based on the overarching questions, the concept defines the criteria to be examined, the necessary data required, and the needed methods and instruments for data collection. The document ideally includes a timeline (see section 3.3.4) and a list of the committee members (see section 3.2).

3.3.1
Criteria for the Evaluation of Centers
To assess the quality of the research and teaching, as well as any services provided by the centers, and their strategic orientation and infrastructural circumstances with respect to the requirements and measures set out in the performance mandates, the following criteria are significant for each self-evaluation:
• strategic overall orientation: positioning in national and international context; contribution to society; implementation of the performance mandate; development possibilities and priorities (possibly referring to previous evaluation results)
• structures (incl. governance and administration)
• research profile: research activities and results in light of available resources and strategic relevance; positioning in the national and international context; cooperation; promotion of young academics; publication strategy
• teaching and promotion of young academics: teaching and learning activities, their results and their connection to current research; organization of teaching operations; graduate schools and other training programs associated with the centers; specific measures and instruments to promote young academics
• services (if part of the performance mandate)
• cross-cutting university topics: sustainability, internationalization, equality and equal opportunities, QSE, communication, and relevant processes and structures
• integration and cooperation at university and faculty level: structural aspects, university and/or faculty action plans
• personnel resources (academic and non-academic): focus on research, service, and teaching activities; personnel profile and development; mobility
• financial resources and infrastructure

These criteria make it possible to refer to all aspects of the performance mandates and to analyze them in a center-specific manner. Therefore, they may differ from the evaluation criteria of other institutions.
Prioritization and weighting of the criteria arise from the objectives defined in the respective evaluation concept. The criteria are not always sharply delineated from one another, and some collected data can, of course, be used for multiple criteria.

3.3.2
Data and Data Sources
Based on the prioritization and weighting of the criteria to be evaluated, it is decided which data and information should be collected and analyzed.
The following list is neither exhaustive nor binding. The respective evaluation committee decides which data and data sources promise the best results and can be procured with a reasonable amount of effort.
• Basic documents: statutes, framework regulations, rules of procedure, performance mandates, strategy papers
• Other data sets: structural reports, annual reports, program evaluation reports
• Statistical data and key figures: research indicators & bibliometric data5, personnel statistics6
• Data to be collected (see section 3.3.3): quantitative and qualitative information from and feedback by researchers, lecturers, external experts (mandatory, see section 3.2), former center members

3.3.3
Methods of Data Collection
Based on the defined evaluation criteria and the data needed for them, the evaluation committee decides on the appropriate methods and instruments for collecting data that are not already available.
In general, the following quantitative and qualitative methods can be considered. The choice of method should be based on both the type of data to be collected and the target groups.
• Surveys (questionnaires)
• Focus group discussions
• Individual interviews
• Retreats or workshops

Other methods and hybrid forms (e.g., representative statements from stakeholder groups that go beyond qualitative comments in surveys) are also possible.

3.3.4
Timeline
Once the evaluation procedure has been conceptualized, the evaluation committees set the processes and their timing in a binding manner and communicate them transparently.

3.4
Data Collection and Analysis
After the conceptual phase is completed, the evaluation procedures are conducted in accordance with the individual evaluation concept and associated timeline. The respective evaluation committee is responsible for the communication measures, oversees the schedules, and coordinates the individual steps. It analyzes and discusses the evaluation results—possibly with further members of the centers—before they are summarized in the final report and action plan.

3.5
Evaluation Report and Action Plan
The final report is the central element of the self-evaluation. Its quality decisively determines the benefit and effectiveness of the entire procedure. It provides an inventory of the current situation of the evaluated center and points out its development possibilities. Since the evaluation results also serve as a basis for decisions on new performance mandates, it is essential that the final report contains as precise reasoning and concretely formulated measures as possible for future development.

3.5.1
Evaluation Report
In a concise but meaningful final report, the evaluation committee documents the evaluation methods, data, and results, as well as the measures serving the development of the center and its framework conditions.
The final report can be created as a text document, which should not exceed 20 pages (plus appendices), or as a slide presentation with about 15–20 individual slides. It must be written in German or English and should preferably be submitted to the QSE Division in electronic (PDF) format.
The report must include the following elements:
• List of committee members and their roles
• One- to two-page summary presenting the key results of the performance period, especially in research, teaching, and promotion of young academics, as well as, if applicable, central critical issues to be addressed in the following report7
• Results and findings according to the criteria in the evaluation concept (see section 3.3.1)
• Action plan (possibly included as an appendix)
• Separate statements by external experts, if they are not peers and members of the evaluation committee (see section 3.2; to be included as appendices)8
• Other relevant appendices (tables, statistics, regulations, additional information)

3.5.2
Action Plan
The aim of evaluation procedures is the substantive and strategic development of the evaluated centers. Therefore, it is necessary to derive targeted measures from the collected data and findings, as concretely as possible with a view to the newly to be formulated performance mandates. SWOT analyses can be used as a preparatory step, in which the evaluation results are summarized in a matrix making it easy to derive the majority of actions to be decided upon (see Appendix 2).
The involvement of all status groups represented in the evaluation committees in the discussion of measures is a prerequisite to ensuring that all those concerned, or at least the majority, consider the measures to be realistic and sensible, and support them.
The action plan should take the following aspects into account:
• Justification of the measures, description of problems
• Identification of the measures
• Responsibility for implementation
• Implementation period or deadline
• Priority
• Resource requirements
• Effectiveness indicators to assess the implementation status

3.5.3
Approval Procedure
The evaluation committee agrees on the final version of the final report and the action plan contained therein and, if necessary, submits both to the center management for approval. Afterwards, the dean and the Q-officer of the organizationally assigned faculty have the opportunity to review and comment on the documents9. With a notation confirming their awareness, the report documents are then preferably submitted electronically to the WiMa responsible for evaluation procedures in the QSE Division.

3.6
Report Analysis and New Performance Mandate
The results of the evaluation procedures are analyzed in the QSE Division on the basis of the submitted final reports and summarized for the UL. After a positive assessment of the evaluation results and the derived measures, the UL instructs the UL staff to draw up the new performance mandates in cooperation with the center managements and the QSE Division and (optionally) the Q-officers of the faculties involved.
If there are clear weaknesses or deficiencies regarding the fulfillment of performance mandates or significant structural problems, the QSE Division, in consultation with the Vice-Rector Quality, may take an intermediate step to develop specific measures for inclusion in the performance mandate together with the center management and/or the evaluation committee. In rare cases and after careful examination, the Vice-Rector Quality may submit a request for an external (third-party) evaluation to the UL.

3.7
Communication
Transparent communication before, during, and after completion of the evaluation procedures ensures acceptance and support from all participating status groups.
At the latest by the end of the conceptual phase (see section 3.3), the respective evaluation committee informs about the significance, key elements, and dates of the evaluation procedure. During the course of the procedure, it makes sense to communicate the start and end of individual steps (conducting surveys, drafting the final report) and the conclusion of the process and the main results. Moreover, the center management informs the main participating faculty in an appropriate way10 about the completion and main results of the self-evaluation.

3.8
Follow-up Reporting
The forms and cycles of follow-up reporting are set out in the performance mandates.

3.8.1
Strategy Meetings
As part of annual strategy meetings, the center managements report to the UL—using a reporting template defined by the UL staff—on the status of implementation of the services and measures agreed in the performance mandates.
In the fourth year of each performance period, during which the centers conduct self-evaluations in preparation for new performance mandates, no strategy meetings take place.

3.8.2
Status Discussion (Follow-up)
During the first half of the second year of the current performance period (ideally by the end of the spring semester), the Vice-Rector Quality and the WiMa responsible for the evaluation procedures in the QSE Division hold a meeting with the center managements11. In this meeting, the previous evaluation procedures are reviewed, the new performance mandates are discussed with regard to QSE-relevant aspects and actions for the centers and their implementation, and any other QSE-related topics are considered.
The subject of the status discussions is not the substantive reporting on the various service areas. It is an open exchange in support of the centers’ work.

Appendix 2: SWOT Analysis Template (see section 3.5.2)
The SWOT analysis is a standardized instrument used in organizational development. It serves to determine position and inform strategy, and can also be used in the university context. Both an internal and an external perspective are taken on the subject, and various criteria and data are related in a matrix:
• Strengths: View of one’s own (current) strengths and abilities
• Weaknesses: View of one’s own (current) weaknesses
• Opportunities: View of (future) conditions with positive potential
• Threats: View of (future) conditions with negative potential

These four categories are related to each other in four different strategic directions. Various combinations and thus strategic objectives are developed.

In the case of center evaluations, the results and findings obtained—both quantitatively and qualitatively—can be assigned to the four categories and sorted according to relevance for the center being evaluated. The three to five (the number should be the same for all categories) most important identified strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats are then related to one another as per the schema, so that strategic objectives can be formulated. Raise any need for support or advice.

1 The AKKRED supports the University Management, the central administration, the faculties, and the strategic centers in planning and reviewing the implementation of the university strategy.  
2 In collaboration with the organizational units of the central administration, the AKKRED reviews and updates the University Action Plan annually with measures for the ongoing planning of the implementation of the sub-strategies within the area of competency of the University Management.

1 Introduction
Outstanding achievements in teaching are recognized and appreciated at the University of Bern. The basis for this recognition is the results of standardized course evaluations. The criteria for classifying a course as "outstanding" were developed by the Vice Rectorate for Quality and Sustainable Development in cooperation with the faculties. The recognition of outstanding achievements in teaching (ALL) in the form of a diploma is intended as an incentive for good teaching and to motivate lecturers to further improve their teaching. Furthermore, this provides especially the mid-level academic staff1 with the opportunity to distinguish themselves in teaching and to present an official confirmation of positive achievements when applying for positions.
Further information about the ALL award can be found in the university document "Concept for the Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching (ALL)". Based on Article 36 paragraph 1 letter m of the Law on the University dated September 5, 1996 (UniG), as well as Article 21 paragraph 1 letter m, Article 29 paragraph 1 letter b number 2, and Article 30 of the Statutes of the University of Bern dated June 7, 2011 (University Statutes, UniSt),

Art. 1  
These Rules of Procedure regulate the status, the tasks, and the organization of the Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (hereinafter referred to as QSE Commission).

Art. 2  
1 The QSE Commission is a standing commission at the University of Bern as defined in Article 29 paragraph 1 letter b number 2 UniSt.  
2 It is administratively assigned to the Vice-Rectorate Quality. This office is responsible for the planning, preparation, and follow-up of the commission meetings as well as the drafting of the meeting minutes.

Art. 3  
1 The QSE Commission advises the University Executive Board on issues of quality assurance and quality development.  
2 The QSE Commission  
a) develops basic documents to ensure quality assurance and quality development at the overall university level and at faculty level, in accordance with the corresponding university, cantonal, and national guidelines and taking into account the diversity of subject or faculty cultures,  
b) issues rules of procedure and submits them to the Senate for approval,  
c) advises the University Executive Board concerning the design and further development of the university quality assurance system,  
d) handles further business concerning university QSE assigned to it by the University Executive Board.

1 A decision requires a simple majority of the voting Commission members present.  
2 The Chair does not vote. In the event of a tie, the Chair has the casting vote.  
3 Decisions are made by open vote.

1 Matters that cannot be postponed may be handled by circular resolution. Members must be given an assessment period of at least 15 working days for these matters. A decision requires a majority of all voting members and must be recorded in the minutes of the next meeting. If the quorum is not reached or at least three Commission members request it, the matter is placed on the agenda for the next Commission meeting and is reassessed and decided there.  
2 The Chair decides whether a matter can be handled by circular resolution.

Art. 12  
1 Minutes of the Commission meetings are taken under the responsibility of the Chair, and these are submitted for approval at the next meeting.  
2 The minutes include the voting results and the wording of the decisions taken. Individual votes are only recorded if the voter explicitly requests it for the record.

Art. 3  
1 AKKRED supports the University Executive Board, Central Services, the faculties, and the strategic centers in planning and reviewing the implementation of the university strategy.

a for the rolling planning of the implementation of the performance mandates,  
b for the implementation of the sub-strategies within the area of competence of the University Executive Board (connection with the University Action Plan),  
c from the minutes of the strategy talks of the previous year.

c notifying possible discussion points to the staff of the University Executive Board ahead of the annual strategy talks with the centers.

The Swiss Accreditation Council has accredited the University of Bern as a higher education institution without any conditions.

With the accreditation decision received by the University of Bern on September 24, 2021, the process of institutional accreditation could be concluded (further information in the procedural report [https://aaq.ch/download/universitaet-bern-unibe/]).

According to the Federal Act on the Promotion and Coordination of the Swiss Higher Education Sector [https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2014/691/de], accreditation is a prerequisite for the University of Bern to continue to call itself a university and to receive the federal contributions provided for in the law.

The basis for the positive accreditation decision was a self-assessment report prepared by the University of Bern in 2020 [https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e809/e810/e812/e708931/e715528/e1059275/Selbstbeurteilungsbericht2020_ger.pdf] and the evaluation by external experts in spring 2021. In their report, the experts awarded the university a very good rating. With their analyses and assessments, they concluded that the University of Bern has an efficient quality assurance system that covers all areas and processes of the university. Particularly positive mention was given by the experts to the successful participation of all faculties, representative bodies, and central administrative staff in the development, implementation, and execution of the QSE processes.

The accreditation is valid for seven years.

Read the article and the corresponding interview with Prof. Silvia Schroer in the unilink magazine (issue 03/2021) [https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e809/e810/e812/e708931/e715528/e1153738/Unilink_November_2021_DEF_ger.pdf]. Centers and Interfaculty Units

Strategic Centers
Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics (AEC)

The Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics was established in 2011 to promote high-level research and teaching in fundamental physics at the University of Bern. The focus is on experimental and theoretical particle physics, but it also includes interdisciplinary activities, such as medical applications. It is also strongly committed to public outreach. The center connects the Institute of Theoretical Physics and the Laboratory for High Energy Physics (LHEP) and works with other members of the science and medical faculties. With over 120 members, the AEC is one of the largest university groups for particle physics in Switzerland and a strong international player in science and education.
To the website (https://www.einstein.unibe.ch/index_eng.html)

ARTORG Center for Biomedical Engineering Research

The ARTORG Center produces innovative health technology through the combination of engineering and medical know-how. Multidisciplinary research groups develop cost-efficient solutions to challenges in preventive medicine, diagnostics, therapy, management of chronic diseases, as well as rehabilitation and telemedicine. Through inventiveness, diversity, and teamwork at the heart of the Medical Faculty, ARTORG puts its expertise in the simulation of physiological processes, biomechanics, and intelligent technologies at the service of healthcare professionals and, through its own startups and industry partners, directly contributes to improved patient care.
To the website (https://www.artorg.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Bern Center for Precision Medicine (BCPM)

The Bern Center for Precision Medicine (BCPM) was founded in 2019 as an initiative of the canton, the university, and the Insel Gruppe, and is active in science, education, and networking. The center is dedicated to precision medicine methods by promoting the research and development of new treatments and therapies. It provides an interdisciplinary platform for researchers and clinicians from various fields and faculties. It trains the next generation of medical doctors, nurses, and scientists and realizes the long-term benefits of precise healthcare.
To the website (https://www.bcpm.unibe.ch/)

Center for Regional Economic Development (CRED)

The Center for Regional Economic Development (CRED) is an interfaculty center for research, teaching, and consulting on questions of regional economic development. The center brings together a group of scholars from the research units in economics, entrepreneurship, geography, and tourism, who address questions in the following research areas: (1) location dynamics and regional economic policy, (2) tourism, and (3) land policy and real estate. CRED is a scientific partner for regional, cantonal, national, and international public and private organizations.
To the website (https://www.cred.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Center for Space and Habitability (CSH)

The Center for Space and Habitability (CSH) aims to promote dialogue and interaction between the various scientific disciplines concerned with the formation, discovery, and characterization of other worlds within and beyond the solar system, our search for life in other parts of the universe, and the impacts on disciplines outside of the natural sciences. We are actively committed to equality of gender, origin, religion, etc., and engage in dialogue with different audiences.
To the website (https://www.csh.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Centre for Development and Environment (CDE)

The CDE is the center of competence for sustainable development. In the spirit of engaged and transformative science, we place our research and teaching at the service of a sustainable future. Our goal is to develop paths to sustainable development in Switzerland and internationally and to initiate change processes that are innovative, concrete, and anchored in each context. We study the interplay between natural resources, land use and ecosystems, their social and political regulation, as well as living conditions and human well-being. At the University of Bern, we are tasked with embedding sustainability university-wide in research and teaching.
To the website (https://www.cde.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Kompetenzzentrum für Public Management (KPM)

The Kompetenzzentrum für Public Management at the University of Bern was established in 2002. It is a nationally and internationally recognized research center in administrative science. As an interfaculty unit of the Faculty of Business, Economics and Social Sciences and the Faculty of Law, it is committed to interdisciplinary collaboration between law, economics, and political science. The center's core products are basic research in the management of public institutions and tasks, the Public Management & Policy (PMP) master’s program, as well as the Executive Master of Public Administration (Executive MPA) and the CAS course "Management und Politik öffentlicher Institutionen" (CeMaP). In addition, services for the public sector are provided in the form of analyses, expert opinions, and evaluations.
To the website (https://www.kpm.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Multidisciplinary Center for Infectious Diseases (MCID)

The Multidisciplinary Center for Infectious Diseases (MCID) is a strategic center at the University of Bern, founded in 2021 through a generous donation from Stiftung Vinetum. MCID is dedicated to researching and containing health, social, ethical, and economic risks posed by infectious diseases and is engaged in research, teaching, and public outreach. It brings together top academic researchers from the University of Bern, the Inselspital, and associated institutes. The MCID is organized into seven interconnected scientific discipline clusters, with three core activities at its center: the BEready cohort, the BioPreparedness BioBank, and the Ethics and Policy Lab.
To the website (https://www.mcid.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

Oeschger Centre for Climate Change Research (OCCR)

The Oeschger Centre for Climate Change Research (OCCR) is a leading climate research center, bringing together researchers from 14 institutes and four faculties. The OCCR conducts interdisciplinary research at the forefront of climate science. The Oeschger Centre was founded in 2007 and is named after Hans Oeschger (1927–1998), a pioneer of modern climate research who worked in Bern. The OCCR has around 300 members. Their research focuses on the impacts of climate change on humans and ecosystems and deals intensively with the social consequences of this unprecedented change.
To the website (https://www.oeschger.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

World Trade Institute (WTI)

The World Trade Institute (WTI) is a leading academic institution engaged in education, research, and outreach at the international level on issues of global trade regulations and investment. As a center of competence at the University of Bern, we study the interrelationships between law, economics, and political science. We recruit students, researchers, faculties, and staff from all over the world to create a diverse culture and stimulating environment. With an established global partner network, the WTI is at the forefront of interdisciplinary research in international trade regulation and investment. We investigate, collect data, and provide policy recommendations on how trade is linked to issues such as migration, environment/climate change, investment, agriculture/food security, energy, raw materials, and development.
