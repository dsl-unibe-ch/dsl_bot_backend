General & Background Information

What are the purposes of course evaluation?

Course evaluation provides concrete indications for the improvement of teaching/learning situations and enables teaching quality at the University of Bern to be maintained at a high level and further developed. In addition, it serves as proof of quality to legislators and the public. Read more about the purposes of course evaluation here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#e1121856).

Which courses and assessment elements are evaluated?

The quality officers of the faculties are responsible for the selection and registration of courses and assessments to be evaluated. The minimum requirements according to the framework concept are: every course should be evaluated at least once every three years; 50% of all assessments should be evaluated at least once every three years; for new lecturers and those in qualification positions, final evaluation is mandatory. More on mandatory evaluations and cycle can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933).

How often are evaluations conducted?

Mid-term evaluation: no prescribed rhythm
Final evaluation: every course should be evaluated at least once every three years
Assessment evaluation: 50% of all assessments should be evaluated at least once within three years
Read more about this here: Cycle (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933); Evaluation timing (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121958).

What do the questionnaires look like?

Sample questionnaires for mid-term evaluation, final evaluation, and assessment evaluation can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#section1122024). Sample supplementary modules (optional additional blocks of questions) can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1101959).

Is it possible to customize a questionnaire?

Individual customization of the standard questionnaires is not possible. For the final evaluation, optional additional blocks of questions (supplementary modules) are available, which can be added to the core questionnaire as needed. The sample supplementary modules can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1101959). Moreover, faculties may design their own survey instruments. The Course Evaluation Office can provide feedback on individually designed questionnaires, but implementation and analysis cannot be done centrally. Custom questionnaires cannot replace mandatory final evaluation with the standard questionnaire.

In what languages are the questionnaires available?

The questionnaires for the mid-term, final evaluation, and assessment evaluation are available in German, English, and French (language selection using the flag symbol at the top of the survey).

How many students must participate in a survey at minimum?

To preserve student anonymity, the results of a survey are only automatically sent if at least 5 students have participated. In exceptional cases, instructors can receive results upon request if fewer than 5 but more than 2 students participated. Results of assessment evaluations are only sent if at least 5 students participated.

Why should students participate in the surveys?

Course evaluation is a tool that measures to what extent the conditions and target criteria for good teaching have been met. This measurement is based on students’ survey ratings and is therefore only possible if students participate. Students benefit in two ways: instructors and the university use the results to further develop teaching – improvements that then benefit students in their courses and assessments. Surveys also offer students a chance to reflect on their own behavior and contribution to learning success and improve in the future if necessary. When a course is evaluated, full participation from all students is important for a representative result. Read more about the purposes of evaluation here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

How can students participate in a survey?

Final and mid-term evaluation: Instructors provide students with a QR code in class (usually on the second to last session) that leads to the survey. Alternatively, a survey link can be provided (e.g., by email or ILIAS). Assessment evaluation: Students receive a link to the survey by email from the Course Evaluation Office.

Why do students have to participate in so many surveys?

The number and selection of courses and assessments to be evaluated each semester are set by the quality officers of the respective faculty. Some faculties evaluate many or all courses and assessments, so it is possible students must complete a relatively high number of surveys. Read more about the frequency and cycle here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121933).

Are the surveys anonymous?

Yes, all surveys are anonymous. There is no way to trace who gave which answers.

Why aren’t evaluation registrations in KSL copied over to the new semester?

There is no automation, to avoid administrative idle time. At the start of a semester, there should be a deliberate decision to conduct an evaluation, which someone is responsible for. For a course with multiple instructors, are there separate surveys for each person or a joint survey for the whole course?

That depends on how the instructors teach. If they teach independently of each other, individual feedback for each is meaningful. In that case, select the corresponding instructors in KSL, so that a separate survey is created for each. Note that students must complete a separate survey for each individually evaluated instructor – thus, a maximum of three instructors should be evaluated individually per course. If instructors act as a team, you can select the “team evaluation” option for joint feedback on the whole team. For assessment evaluation: one survey per assessment is created. Either an individual survey for the main responsible person or a team evaluation if several instructors are jointly responsible.

Should the evaluation be done online or on paper?

Since the spring semester 2022, all surveys for final, mid-term, and assessment evaluation are conducted online. The advantages of online surveys are numerous: instructors and admin staff save on printing and scanning; results are provided automatically and immediately after survey closure; online surveys allow for multiple language versions and filter questions; for surveys with personalized TAN links, automatic reminders are possible; and online surveys are sustainable, saving around 100,000 sheets of paper per semester.

What data collection methods are available for course evaluation?

A distinction is made between surveys with personalized and non-personalized links. Surveys for mid-term and final evaluations are usually conducted with non-personalized links (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/schlussevaluation/index_ger.html#pane1127465) ("Losung"). Surveys for assessment evaluation are always conducted with personalized links (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/evaluation_der_leistungskontrolle/index_ger.html#pane1065968) ("TAN").

Why was no survey set up for a course?

If no survey was set up for a course, it was presumably not registered in KSL for that evaluation. In that case, contact the Course Evaluation Office to check if a survey can still be set up.

Which students are eligible to participate in an online survey?

Final and mid-term evaluation: the instructor distributes the survey link to eligible students via email, posts it on ILIAS, or shows the QR code on a presentation slide. Can a course be evaluated if it has fewer than 5 participants?

Standard surveys are recommended for courses with 5 or more participants. For data protection reasons, results from surveys (mid-term or final) with fewer than 5 participants are only sent on request. Results with 5 or more respondents are included in the faculty average. Surveys can be created for smaller courses, but there is no guarantee the results can be accessed. Assessments are only evaluated using the standard method when there are 5 or more participants. If there are fewer than 5, no results are provided. For exams with small participant numbers, faculties should use other, qualitative evaluation methods.

How can you tell which instructors and which course are being evaluated with the given questionnaire?

Final or mid-term evaluation: instructors receive the results immediately after the survey closes. A PDF report with automatic analysis of the survey results and student comments is sent by email. The Course Evaluation Office does not grant students access to evaluation results. However, instructors should discuss survey results and any planned improvements with students wherever possible. The results are not published by the Course Evaluation Office. However, it is possible individual faculties may publish internal results through their own channels.

Should the results be discussed with students?

To close the first feedback loop, the framework concept stipulates that instructors should discuss the survey results, key findings, and planned improvements with students before the end of the semester. This demonstrates to students that instructors are engaging with the feedback.

How do I interpret the results report?

The detailed report for the final evaluation includes an overall result, from which you can derive which success level (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121983) has been achieved in the evaluation. It also includes the individual results for the indicators “Learning progress” and “Satisfaction” and the results for each question (distribution of responses as well as mean and standard deviation for scale questions). Then the answers to the open questions are listed. The results for the mid-term evaluation and assessment evaluation are not combined into an overall result. For these evaluations, individual questions or indicators can be interpreted in the context of the course or assessment. The Course Evaluation Office can be involved at any time for help interpreting results. Sample reports can be viewed here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#section1122015).

Interim Feedback  
How are your students managing with the course? What difficulties in understanding are there? What can be changed to promote student learning?

With interim feedback, you find answers to questions about further developing your teaching.

Background

Quality Assurance (https://lead.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/zwischenfeedback/index_ger.html#collapse_pane971338)

Course evaluations are a fixed component of the quality assurance and quality development concept at the University of Bern. Regular so-called interim evaluations, as well as final evaluations and assessments of performance reviews are carried out using standardized instruments. To appreciate and promote formative evaluation, voluntary interim feedback has been integrated as an additional element into the framework concept for course evaluation at the university (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html). Interim feedback does not follow a defined process and there are no specific guidelines. It is a quick and uncomplicated form of feedback that usually requires little preparation.

Benefits  
Interim feedback is important for the development of teaching and for student learning for three reasons:

- As an instructor, you are free to choose which aspects of teaching and learning you want to focus on in your interim feedback.
- Adjustments to teaching based on interim feedback can have a motivating effect on your students’ engagement.
- If you address questions from the final evaluation during the interim feedback and discuss them with students, this can have a positive effect on the evaluation culture and the results of the final evaluation.

Planning and Implementation  
Timing  
We recommend that you collect interim feedback no later than by the second third of the semester, so that the results can be discussed with students and any necessary adjustments can still be made during the current course. For specific feedback, such as on a particular method or concrete input, a later point in the semester can also be considered.

Evaluation  
The big advantage of interim feedback is the opportunity to ask students directly and obtain concrete suggestions. Provide feedback to the students, especially about which points in the feedback stood out to you, what you found helpful, and what changes you will implement. It is often very helpful to note what will not be changed for didactic reasons. In this way, you encourage students to reflect on their own contributions to the success of the course and their own learning outcomes.

You can find further assessment methods for determining your students' learning progress here: www.assessment.unibe.ch (http://www.assessment.unibe.ch/). Interim Evaluation  
The interim evaluation is student feedback that is collected using a standardized questionnaire. It is a service provided by the LVE office and is conducted through the central evaluation system evasys. The interim evaluation allows instructors to gather students’ opinions and suggestions at an early stage of the course, in order to improve the course accordingly during the semester itself.

  
Possible Uses of the Interim Evaluation  
VOLUNTARY

The interim evaluation is generally voluntary.

AS A FOLLOW-UP

For courses that received the rating "insufficient" in a final evaluation, the interim evaluation is mandatory the next time the course is conducted.

  
Purposes of the Interim Evaluation  
For more on the purposes of course evaluation, see also here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

1) Further Development, Improvement

The interim evaluation is purely formative, i.e., intended for improvement. Its purpose is to provide instructors with detailed data-based indications of areas where the course needs improvement (see Model of Teaching Quality (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1027399/e1027402/VRQ_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_md3_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_ger.jpg)).

2) Preparation for the Final Evaluation

The interim evaluation is a valuable preparation for the final evaluation. Since the interim evaluation directly addresses the quality dimensions that constitute good teaching, it supports instructors in identifying potential areas for improvement. If measures are subsequently taken to optimize these dimensions, this will be reflected in the next final evaluation, as it will be based on the interim evaluation.

3) Fostering a Feedback Culture

Furthermore, the interim evaluation can help foster an open feedback culture within a course.

Please note that an interim evaluation does not replace the final evaluation.

  
Timing of the Interim Evaluation  
Standard implementation: in the first half of the course sessions, by no later than the 9th week of the semester (depending on the schedule)

  
Data Collection Method  
The surveys for the interim evaluation are conducted online with a non-personalized link (password/code). The link and QR code are sent to the main instructor or, in some cases, to an administrative office in the faculty and then made available to the students.

  
Content, Languages  
CONTENT

The data collection tool for the interim evaluation is based on the Model of Teaching Quality (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1027399/e1027402/VRQ_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_md3_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_ger.jpg) and is structured as follows:

Dimension Instructor (conveying the subject, specifying learning outcomes, interaction with students, suggestions relevant to learning status)  
Dimension Course (content and structure, support for learning via tools, level of challenge: over- or under-challenging, pace)  
Dimension Students (active participation, structured learning)

More information here under Content, Languages (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/zwischenevaluation/index_ger.html#collapse_pane1065609)

  
LANGUAGES

The questionnaire is available in German, English, and French (selectable by flag icon in the survey).

  
Process of the Interim Evaluation  
1. Planning  
In an initial step, instructors schedule 2–3 times for the survey while preparing the course: one date for administration, possibly a reserve date, and one date for discussing the results with the students.

2. Registration  
Registration usually takes place until the 3rd week of the semester in KSL (see Deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/zwischenevaluation/%7B$uid:b3445c2b-6af9-4547-80b3-39a8769286b2%7D)). Register the interim evaluation in KSL.

Courses that must conduct a mandatory interim evaluation due to a negative final evaluation are registered automatically, or by a central office of the institute.

3. Creation of the Survey  
Four weeks before the mid-point of the course (or immediately, if the registration occurs later), registrations are transferred to the evaluation system evasys and the surveys are set up.

4. Distribution of the Link  
An email with the link and QR code to the survey is sent 1–3 weeks before the planned end date of the interim evaluation to the main responsible instructor, or to alternative recipients upon request.

This email contains the planned survey end date and additional information on the process.

5. Conducting the Survey  
The interim evaluation should take place during the first half of the course sessions.

As soon as the responsible person receives the link or QR code, the survey can be conducted. Ideally, about 10 minutes should be allotted to students at the start of a class session.

To increase response rates, it is recommended to conduct surveys in the (possibly virtual) presence of the instructor(s).

The survey is automatically closed at the date specified during registration or a date determined by the LVE office.

6. Report  
Immediately after the survey closes, all instructors of the evaluated course receive an automatic summary report, provided a minimum of 5 responses has been reached.

With fewer than 5 responses, instructors receive an automatic message indicating that the report could not be generated. Upon request to the LVE office (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch), depending on the response rate, the office can manually forward the report or at least the answers to open questions.

For questions regarding interpretation of the results, instructors can contact the LVE office (lehrevaluation@unibe.ch).

For questions regarding university didactics, the LEAD department (https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/hochschuldidaktik_und_lehrentwicklung/) is available for advisory support.

7. Discussion  
It is recommended to discuss the results of the survey, as well as insights gained and potential consequences (e.g., improvement measures), with the students.

8. Adjustments  
Student feedback may give reason to make adjustments to the course.

9. Follow-Up  
Course evaluation is designed according to the Model of Teaching Quality (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121880) so that the questionnaires for interim and final evaluation are coordinated in terms of content. This means a final evaluation following an interim evaluation can reveal the extent to which improvement measures have been successfully implemented in the course. Therefore, it is advisable to use interim and final evaluations in combination.

Application possibilities for the evaluation of assessment performance

The standard questionnaire (https://scanserveruls.unibe.ch/evasys/online.php?p=7JDG6) can primarily be used for the evaluation of written and oral exams that are directly related to a course and have a minimum of 5 participants.

Alternatively, faculties can use their own methods for evaluating assessments (e.g., focus group discussions), especially in examinations with very few participants and for exam formats that are not written or oral exams.

Furthermore, lecturers are encouraged to use online-based tools (https://www.assessment.unibe.ch/) as well as courses and consultations from the Learning and Development department (https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch/dienstleistungen/hochschuldidaktik_und_lehrentwicklung/) to support exam preparation and review.

REGULAR

The framework concept stipulates that 50% of all assessments must be evaluated regularly (at least every 3 years). The faculties plan the evaluations accordingly and select the assessments to be evaluated, taking into account the faculty QSE guidelines.

OPTIONAL

Lecturers can voluntarily register their assessments for evaluation.

Purposes of evaluation of assessment performance

Assessments (exams) play a central role in the teaching and learning routine at universities as they steer teaching and guide the learning behavior of students. For this reason, the evaluation of assessments (ELK) is highly valuable for teaching design and development.

The ELK provides lecturers and the faculty examination boards with data-based indications of what can be improved in the assessments – and beyond, in the courses themselves (improvement orientation). ELK also serves the purpose of accountability to legislators and the public.

More information about the purposes of evaluation can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#pane1121855).

Timing of the evaluation of assessment performance

Standard implementation time: After the assessment has taken place (usually at 8:00 p.m. on the day of the exam), from the 12th week of the semester onwards.

Unless otherwise agreed, the surveys are conducted before the marks are announced. The evaluation results are only sent to the lecturers after the grades have been finalized. Special attention is paid to data protection compliance.

The standardized questionnaire (https://scanserveruls.unibe.ch/evasys/online.php?p=7JDG6) for the evaluation of assessments (oral and written exams) is based on the teaching quality model (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1027399/e1027402/VRQ_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_md3_Modell_Lehrqualitaet_ger.jpg) and covers the following dimensions:

- Exam conditions (5.1-5.2)
- Difficulty of the exam (6.1).

"Fairness" of an exam and the mark itself are not the subject of this evaluation.

Procedure for the evaluation of assessment performance

1. Registration
Evaluations should be registered in the KSL system by the 10th week of the semester (see deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html#e855666)). Usually, registration is carried out by a central office of the institute.

In order to register an evaluation of an assessment in KSL, the following information must be available:
•    Exam date(s)
•    Deadlines for registration and de-registration for the exam

Evaluating multiple exam dates is possible, but only one date per assessment is recommended for evaluation.

Early evaluations that are to take place before the standard period (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html) must, in addition to registration in KSL, be confirmed by the LVE unit via email.

Participant lists with at least 5 students should be available for the respective exam date. They may also be saved to KSL at a later time, but not later than one day before the exam. Assessment evaluations with fewer than 5 registrations will not be set up in the evaluation system.

2. Creation of surveys
After the registration deadline, the LVE unit prepares the ELK surveys.

3. Information
Lecturers receive an automatic email about the start of the survey as soon as students have received the link to the survey.

The LVE unit does not inform lecturers in advance that an evaluation is planned.

4. Data collection
Students receive an email with the access link to the questionnaire at 8:00 p.m. on the day of the assessment.

The surveys remain open for 24 hours (oral assessments) or 4 days (written assessments and all other types) and close automatically. For surveys that last 4 days, a reminder is sent after 2 days to students who have not yet completed the survey.

5. Announcement of grades
After the survey has closed, lecturers or responsible persons can enter the grades in KSL and forward them to the dean's office for approval.

Lecturers are free to set the grades earlier, but should not announce them to students until afterward (unless another arrangement has been made with the LVE unit or it is an exceptional case – e.g., if exchange students need their grade sooner).

Lecturers then confirm by email to the LVE unit that the grades have been entered and forwarded to the dean's office.

6. Validity check
After the LVE unit has received confirmation that grades have been entered, it checks the response rate and the number of valid responses to maintain student anonymity.

7. Report
If the response rate is sufficient (min. 5 valid responses), the report will be sent to the lecturers. If the response rate is too low, no report will be generated.

If confirmation of grade announcement is missing, reports will only be sent to lecturers after individually agreed deadlines with the faculties (e.g., 6 weeks after the last exam).

8. Sharing the results
It is recommended to share the evaluation results and insights gained with students (e.g., via ILIAS or by email).

9. Adjustments
If necessary, the lecturers make adjustments to the didactic concept of the course and/or the assessment.

10. Follow-up
The faculty examination board or a board or unit responsible for these tasks analyzes the results, develops recommendations, and takes appropriate measures.

The results of the ELK can also feed into the evaluation of study programs / courses of study.

Framework Concept
The framework concept (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1009019/e1009041/Rahmenkonzept_2024_Final_ger.pdf) for the implementation and use of course evaluations provides an overview of the measures for quality assurance and development (QSE) at the level of individual courses. The most important points of the framework concept are described on this page.

Purposes of the Evaluations
Course evaluation at the University of Bern serves multiple purposes. It is carried out in the form of student surveys and thus involves students in the design of teaching (participation rights). The resulting data provides concrete indications for the improvement of teaching and learning situations (improvement orientation). Finally, it serves as a proof of quality for legislators and the public (accountability).

1) Proof of Quality (Accountability)

As universities fulfill a publicly funded and legally regulated educational mandate, they are obliged to make the implementation and achievement of the goals of their offered services (in this case, the courses) transparent to legislators and the public.

The evaluation of courses is an important component of the system for quality assurance and development at a university and thus a prerequisite for successful institutional accreditation. Only accredited universities are allowed to continue to call themselves "university" and qualify for federal funding.

A functioning system for the evaluation of courses and performance assessments is mandated by quality standards 3.2 and 3.4 of the Higher Education Promotion and Coordination Act (HFKG) (https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2014/691/de).

In addition, the evaluation serves internal monitoring and accountability to the university administration.

More about this:

Accreditation at UniBE (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/institutionelle_akkreditierung/index_ger.html)

Swiss Accreditation Council SAR (https://akkreditierungsrat.ch/akkreditierung/)

Quality Standards HFKG for Institutional Accreditation (https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2015/362/de#sec_6)

2) Further Development (Improvement Orientation)

The evaluation results are used for the further development of teaching. The goal is to identify strengths and weaknesses in teaching in order to generate ideas for modifications.

The results of the evaluations (interim, final, and evaluation of performance assessments) are used to improve teaching on two levels:

- Instructors use the results to further develop their own concepts (learning outcomes, teaching/learning activities, exams).
- Faculties and institutes use the results to optimize prerequisites and framework conditions for good teaching.

3) Participation Rights

By participating in the surveys, students have the opportunity to communicate their opinions about courses and performance assessments and are thus involved in the design of teaching. Participation rights are anchored in the quality standards for institutional accreditation (quality standard 1.3).

Model of Teaching Quality
For course evaluation to fulfill its purposes, it must answer the following question:

To what extent is good teaching implemented in the courses at the University of Bern?

But what does "good teaching" mean?

Based on findings from teaching and learning research, teaching is considered "good" when three decisive factors are well aligned ("didactic coherence," English "constructive alignment"):

- the intended learning outcomes
- the teaching and learning activities
- the assessments of learning outcomes

In line with these findings and the multidimensional model of teaching quality by Rindermann (2009), a model of teaching quality has been developed at the University of Bern. This model reflects the quality-relevant dimensions of a course and a performance assessment, defines the target criteria and their relationships (see illustration).

The model is the basis for our survey instruments.

The data obtained in the surveys show in which areas there is potential for improvement for the respective course or assessment.

More information about the model of teaching quality can be found here (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/rahmenkonzept/index_ger.html#collapse_pane1121880)

Methodology, Sample Questionnaires, Content and Intentions
Methodology
Course evaluation at the University of Bern is based on student feedback—a valid means of assessing course quality (e.g., Treischl & Wolbring, 2017; Fondel et al., 2015; Spooren et al., 2013; Marsh, 2007).

Frequency of Evaluations
Evaluations follow a regular cycle set out in the framework concept:

Final Evaluation

The minimum requirement is that every course is evaluated at least once every three years. Within this framework, individual faculties plan their evaluations according to their own QSE guidelines (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/index_ger.html#e718012) and monitor compliance. For new course formats and newly appointed instructors, the final evaluation is mandatory, as it is for those in qualifying positions (e.g., assistant professorships with tenure track). Courses previously rated as "inadequate" must be re-evaluated the next time they are offered.

Evaluation of Performance Assessment

The minimum requirement is that 50% of all performance assessments are evaluated at least once every 3 years. Faculties plan the evaluations accordingly and select the performance assessments to be evaluated in consideration of their QSE guidelines (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/selbstverstaendnis/qualitaet/index_ger.html#e718012).

Interim Evaluation

For courses rated "inadequate" in the previous evaluation, an interim evaluation must also be conducted the next time they are held.

Instructors may also voluntarily register their course for an interim, final, or performance assessment evaluation.

Data Collection Points
Evaluations are conducted at three different points in the semester.

For registration deadlines and actual evaluation periods for the current semester, see Deadlines (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/fristen/index_ger.html).

Interim Evaluation

- Registration deadline: End of week 3 of semester
- Standard execution date: 1st half of course sessions, by week 9 at the latest (depending on dates)

Final Evaluation

- Registration deadline: End of week 8 of semester
- Standard execution date: Towards the end of the course sessions, from week 11

Evaluation of Performance Assessment

- Registration deadline: End of week 10 of semester
- Standard execution date: From week 12, after performance assessment (usually by 8:00 p.m. on the day of the exam, unless otherwise agreed—before grades are announced)

Criteria and Determination of Levels of Success, Consequences of Actions
The results of the final evaluation are assigned to one of 5 levels of success. Assignment is based on the overall result of a final evaluation. Means from questions 1.1–1.3 (learning progress) and 2.1–2.5 (satisfaction) are taken into account.

Since FS24, the following success levels and thresholds, established together with the faculties and an external expert, apply:

≥5.7 = "excellent" (dark green)
≥5.5 <5.7 = "very good" (light green)

The interim evaluation is not assessed a priori, since it is purely formative. Use of results is at the discretion of the instructors.

QSE in Course Evaluation
Quality assurance and development (QSE) of courses is organized as a control cycle with three interrelated levels:

First Level: Course
Instructors are responsible for QSE at the individual course level. They design the course taking previous evaluation results into account (1). They conduct the course and evaluations, and use the results to draw conclusions for improving the next course and, if applicable, the assessment (2/3). The results and planned changes (4) are communicated to students when possible.

Second Level: Faculty
The faculties develop an evaluation plan (5) and ensure its implementation (6). The faculty’s quality officers receive aggregated results of final evaluations and performance assessments from the LVE office (7) and take corresponding measures (https://www.unibe.ch/studium/werkzeuge_und_arbeitshilfen/fuer_lehrende/lehrveranstaltungsevaluation/massnahmen/index_ger.html) (8), as stipulated in the framework concept and the faculty quality guidelines.

Third Level: University Management
At the end of the academic year, the faculties' quality officers submit a report (https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e1006/e855118/e855497/e946563/e998762/e1124557/Berichterstattung_Vorlage_Semesteruebergreifend_ger.pdf) to the LVE office. They reflect on the evaluation results, put them in context, and report on planned or already undertaken QSE measures.

The LVE office then compiles a summary report, including a short statement on the faculty reports, and communicates the results to university management.

If needed, the results are discussed in operational and strategy meetings (11). If necessary, specific goals are then formulated and measures derived (12), which may, in turn, lead to adjustments to the entire process and/or to the quality strategy (9) and its implementation (10).

You present your students with a short, clear question that interests you for the further development of your course, and record it in writing in a way that is clearly visible to all students. The students answer this question briefly and concisely (no more than two or three sentences) in any order. As the instructor, you note the responses on a flipchart or blackboard and summarize them in terms of content. The Blitzlicht method can also be used as a formative assessment method to learn more about your students' learning progress. The method is suitable for groups of up to 25 students and takes 10-20 minutes, depending on the group size.

Digital alternative: A Blitzlicht can also be very easily used in Zoom, Teams, or similar platforms. The results are ideally recorded via notes on a shared screen. The translated text is:

Ampel Feedback  
The students receive green, yellow, and red cards and indicate their agreement (green card), indecision (yellow card), or disagreement (red card) with a question or statement you have formulated by holding up the appropriate card. Count the voting results directly and record them in writing where everyone can see them clearly (e.g., flipchart, blackboard, visualizer). This method can be used with groups of up to 200 students; for groups larger than 30 people, additional “vote counters” are required. In smaller groups, you can ask individual students to briefly comment on their voting behavior.

Evaluation of Courses and Assessments
Courses represent a significant portion of teaching. With the help of student feedback, a number of relevant questions about courses can be assessed. All instructors’ courses are regularly evaluated. The faculties determine the scope and process of evaluation and monitor the process. In cooperation with the Vice-Rectorate Teaching, they define the average values that must be achieved and ensure the results are used to further develop the courses and teaching in general. The higher education didactics office assists teaching staff in developing their teaching.

The following questions are among those considered relevant:

- Delivery of the subject (definition of learning objectives and structure of the course)
- Engagement of the instructor
- Difficulty and scope of the course
- Student learning progress
- Student engagement
- Background data

Surveys are usually conducted during a course and are anonymous. If the representativeness of the results is guaranteed, online surveys are also possible. During the semester, but no later than the end of the semester, instructors briefly discuss the results with their students and outline planned improvements. The faculties report annually to the university management about the results and any need for action.

Assessments play an important guiding and orientation role in studies. They refer to one or more courses or sections of a program and are conducted orally or in writing. The criteria for evaluating student performance must be defined, communicated, and applied in a systematic, transparent, and consistent manner. The university checks that these criteria are met in the assessments.

Evaluation of Study Programs
The purpose of an evaluation is to determine and document the quality of the study program, its relevance and strategic significance, as well as possibilities for its further development. In addition, such an evaluation is a prerequisite for any potential revision of the curriculum.

The faculty QSE specialist committees, in coordination with the responsible committees, determine which study programs are to be evaluated at what times ("rolling planning"), and communicate this plan faculty-wide. This ensures transparency and planning security. Every study program must be evaluated regularly, and according to national and international standards, a seven to eight year cycle is recommended.

For analysis and assessment, the units rely on quantitative and qualitative data, on surveys and written and oral feedback, and, if necessary, on comparisons.
Key figures and statistics are part of the university’s regular performance measurement. The most important statistics and key figures (https://www.unibe.ch/universitaet/portraet/zahlen__und__fakten/studierende/index_ger.html) are compiled and published by the university’s controlling service. Continuing education at the University of Bern is based on science and research. It promotes the ability of students to make a scientifically reflected transfer of educational content into the professional world and their further living environment.

To achieve this, the University of Bern, in the context of quality assurance and quality development in university continuing education, has formulated its own principles and standards as well as developed instruments. Quality Standards in Continuing Education  
In its understanding of quality, the University of Bern follows the recommendations of Swissuni (http://www.swissuni.ch/qualitaet/) – the organization of continuing education offices at Swiss universities – for quality development in university continuing education. Building upon this, it has formulated the document Guidelines and Standards for Degree Programs in University Continuing Education (https://www.unibe.ch/e152701/e332574/e344586/e351681/wbk_leitfaden_standards_ger.pdf).

Quality Instruments in Continuing Education  
Quality assurance and development in continuing education apply to the entire development and implementation cycle of the programs and are implemented and supported with the following instruments:

- The programs (CAS, DAS, MAS) have study regulations issued by the faculties. Within the approval process, these are checked for compliance with the standards by the Lifelong Learning Center LLC (https://llc.unibe.ch/), the legal services, the continuing education commission, the faculty, the university management, and the senate.
- The Lifelong Learning Center LLC offers comprehensive advice to the providers of continuing education programs, from conception through implementation to evaluation, and has developed many tools such as checklists, process descriptions, and sample templates.
- Those responsible and teaching in university continuing education have access to services in university didactics and teaching development, as well as special events (courses, exchanges of experience) and conferences.
- The basis for evaluation is formed by the evaluation guidelines of the continuing education commission from 2005 and its recommendations for external evaluation from 2012. The Lifelong Learning Center LLC supports the providers in meeting the requirements of the guidelines.

In addition, it has documented its experiences with external evaluation in a report (appendix to the recommendations from 2012).
- The reporting of the Lifelong Learning Center LLC provides an overview of developments in the programs, and monitoring identifies external trends. The findings are incorporated into the renewal of the programs.
- Continuing education will be part of the institution's accreditation of the university in accordance with the accreditation guidelines of the Swiss Higher Education Council. Continuing education has already been included in quality audits. Core Tasks

1.  
Introduction  
 
Quality assurance and quality development (QSE) have been considered a central element of all university areas at the University of Bern even prior to the implementation of the Higher Education Promotion and Coordination Act (HFKG) of 2011. Through the establishment of a holistic quality assurance system encompassing all university performance areas, processes are systematized and appropriate measures for QSE are developed and implemented across all university areas.  
 
The basis for adapting existing or developing new measures for quality assurance and quality development are the quality strategy of the University of Bern and all overarching strategies and resolutions. With the present guideline document, the requirements of the university evaluation regulations are implemented.  
 
 
1.1  
Purpose of the Guidelines  
The present document focuses on the tasks of the university central administration. Its scope thus includes all central university units – the Rectorate, the Vice-Rectorates, and the University Administration Directorate with their assigned sub-units. The guidelines are addressed to the quality officers (Q-Beauftragten) of the Central Administration as well as those responsible for QSE tasks (Q-Verantwortliche) in the units of the Central Administration.  
 
The guidelines describe the principles of QSE at the University of Bern for the tasks and services of the university’s central administration and show how the various elements of QSE are operationalized (organization, communication, etc.). They define framework conditions for reporting and reviewing the services and tasks of the administrative units.  
 
 
1.2  
Foundations and Principles of the Guidelines  
The HFKG defines the requirements for higher education institutions, and institutional accreditation ensures that institutions develop their own quality assurance systems and carry out quality assurance and development according to nationally and internationally valid standards. The law creates the necessary foundation for a competitive, permeable, and high-quality higher education area in Switzerland.  
 
According to Art. 30, paragraph 1a of the HFKG, a prerequisite for institutional accreditation is that the higher education institution has a quality assurance system which includes higher education administration. To implement this, the requirements of the Standards and Guidelines for Quality Assurance in the European Higher Education Area (ESG) as well as the requirements of the university evaluation regulations are taken into account. In accordance with the university strategy 2021, the QSE of the university’s central administration is being expanded.  
 
The following university-wide valid principles are pursued in the guidelines:

•  
Safeguarding a competitive scientific institution  
•  
Ensuring and further developing a high level of quality awareness and thereby establishing a university quality culture  
•  
Creating prerequisites to flexibly and differentiatedly respond to the demands placed on the university  
•  
Involving and assigning responsibility to all university members in accordance with their competencies  
 
Specifically, the guidelines provide orientation and support for the units of the university’s central administration to fulfill the following QSE-relevant criteria in accordance with the university’s quality strategy:

•

1.3  
QSE Organization of the University of Bern and the Central Administration  
The Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission) advises the university management in all matters of quality assurance and quality development. The faculties, the central administration, the intermediate staff, and the students each have a Q-Beauftragten who is a member of the university-wide QSE-Commission. The units organize QSE according to their specific tasks.  
 
The Q-Beauftragte of the central administration is a member of the university QSE-Commission, organizes and coordinates the relevant QSE measures and tasks of the university’s central administration, and is responsible for reporting to the university management. In the respective units of the central administration, the Q-Verantwortliche coordinates the information and data relevant for reporting and, on behalf of the respective management and in consultation with the Q-Beauftragten, organizes the regularly required evaluation procedures (see section 2.1).  
 
The Accreditation Working Group (AKKRED) is an administrative committee, appointed by the university management and entrusted, among other duties, with the task of developing and regularly updating action plans in cooperation with the responsible units as well as the faculties and competence centers. The members of AKKRED have a specific view of ongoing tasks and projects, especially those defined in the university’s quality strategy 2018–2021 (see Fig. 2), such as implementation strategy of the teaching university and the promotion of young talent, and are responsible for the annual QSE reporting (see section 2.2). The working group is composed of staff of the central administration and is managed and coordinated by the QSE Department in the Vice-Rectorate for Quality.

2.  
Governance and Reporting  
 
The central elements of the university QSE system follow the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle. The PDCA cycle describes a continuous development process and is described in greater detail in the university quality strategy. The quality cycle is linked with the governance of the central administration units. Governance of the units lies with the university management.  
 
The most important instruments effective for the central university administration for implementing the QSE cycle are described below.  
 
 
2.1  
Governance and QSE Instruments for the Central Administration  
For the purpose of internal governance, the respective core services of the central administration units are defined by the department heads in cooperation with university management and fixed in the form of service descriptions. In this way, the university ensures that it can respond flexibly and in a differentiated way to the demands placed on it.  
 
Based on the tasks and measures formulated in the university action plan, the department heads, supported by the Q-Verantwortliche, report every four years in the form of self-evaluation reports on the status and implementation of these to university management. At the beginning of a new four-year period, the service descriptions are renewed based on the self-evaluation reports. This model gives the units of the central administration significant latitude for action; it also provides the opportunity to analyze and evaluate the services provided and to consider whether services and measures should be discontinued, supplemented, or newly introduced.  
 
Furthermore, on behalf of the Senate, the suitability and performance of the existing organizational structures in the central administration are evaluated by means of a peer review/external evaluation, focusing on the fulfillment of tasks, resources, and process efficiency, in an eight-year cycle.1  
 
The units of the central administration describe and document their regularly required, standardized activities and services in the form of process modeling and store them in the electronic process documentation. This documentation is kept up to date through an annual process review (see below). The Q-Beauftragte decides who is responsible for coordinating the process review.  
 
 
2.2  
Elements of Annual Reporting  
There are standardized processes for the quality-relevant reporting of the central administration units of the university.  
•  
Feedback cycle for the university action plan: With their annual feedback for updating the university action plan on behalf of the Q-Beauftragte of the central administration to the QSE Department of the Vice-Rectorate for Quality, the responsible members of AKKRED, supported as necessary by the responsible Q-Verantwortliche, report on the implementation of the measures defined in the service description and action plan. The QSE Department filters the information relevant for the management and governance of the university, which is then confirmed by the Q-Beauftragte and prepared for discussion at an annual meeting of the university management. According to the traffic light system provided in the action plan, the implementation of objectives, measures, and services of the central administration units is discussed. Measures assessed as insufficient or not fulfilled, or deemed necessary to be supplemented, are incorporated into the revised version of the action plan.  
•  
Process review: The units of the central administration describe and document their regularly required, standardized activities and services in the form of process modeling. The modeled processes are reviewed annually by the units and updated if necessary. The unit entrusted with this task by the Q-Beauftragte reports on the basis of the process documentation to the Q-Beauftragte of the central administration after the annual review is carried out.

1.1
Principles and Fundamentals
The University of Bern is obliged, on the basis of national legislation, to ensure and document the quality assurance and development (QSE) of its core tasks of teaching, research, and continuing education.1
Regularly conducted evaluations2 of study programs and courses3 are an integral part of the teaching QSE system at the University of Bern. They are used to determine and document the quality of study offerings and content, their relevance and timeliness, as well as opportunities for their further development.
The planning, design, conduct, and analysis of evaluations is the responsibility of the faculties and the evaluating departments and institutes, oriented to university standards and guidelines.4
Study program evaluations (SPE)5 are primarily a QSE instrument that enables systematic reflection on the quality of the study offerings at the level of the evaluating departments and institutes from different perspectives. Quantitative and qualitative data and information regarding structures, content concepts, and organization of the study programs and courses are processed in these evaluations. The evaluations thus serve the aim of providing the best possible education to students by helping to further develop and, where necessary, correct the contents and conditions of study; furthermore, they can contribute to sharpening the profile of a subject, department, or university institute.
In addition, the evaluations are important for the development and positioning of the university as a teaching institution, as well as for faculties, departments, and institutes. In the strategic discussions between university management and the faculties or during structural reporting processes, evaluation results can play a role as planning elements.

2.
Framework Conditions, Organization, and Course of SPE Processes
Faculty management is responsible for the proper and planned execution of the evaluations of study programs and courses. On their behalf, the faculty QSE committees or faculty Quality Officers and WiMa_Q, in consultation with institute and/or program management, determine which study programs are to be evaluated and when. They prepare a medium- and long-term evaluation plan according to the principle of "rolling planning" and communicate this plan throughout the faculty. This creates transparency and planning security. Every study program and course must be evaluated regularly, with a seven- to eight-year cycle being aimed for in accordance with national and international standards (see Annex 1). Interim evaluations (follow-ups) must also be included, in which the effectiveness of decided measures is checked about three years after the conclusion of a SPE process or after the introduction of a new or revised study plan (see ch. 3.9).
Completed evaluation processes are a prerequisite for possible curriculum revisions.8
Every SPE process is fundamentally participatory, since primarily the members of a subject or department are responsible for teaching and its quality, and thus, against the backdrop of scientific, university, and societal requirements, formulate the study programs and plan their implementation and further development. The results of the analysis carried out during the evaluation process allow the study offerings to be discussed and further developed with all stakeholders, with external support as needed.

3.1
Preparation and Planning
Normally, the responsible faculty WiMa_Q9 initiates preparation of the evaluation processes in consultation with the responsible institute and/or program management (if available), according to the rolling evaluation plan.
Preparation should start early, ideally about half a year before the actual start of the evaluation. During this phase, the institute or program directors, possibly jointly with the persons responsible for quality in the evaluated units (Q-responsible), clarify important procedural issues with the faculty WiMa_Q and discuss the procedure (see Annex 2 for a suggested checklist of main aspects and questions that should be clarified when preparing SPE processes).
The institute or program directors also prepare a timeline and a draft of the evaluation concept (see ch. 3.3) and look for suitable members for the evaluation committee (see ch. 3.2). They also check whether financial resources are needed for the evaluation, and at what amount.10
The institute or program directors determine how members and students of the evaluated units will be informed about the upcoming evaluation.
3.2

The term "Evaluation Committee" is generic. Faculties or their evaluating units are free to choose the name of the committee.

The committee decides the evaluation concept (see ch. 3.3) and associated timeline (see ch. 3.3.4). It is responsible for the conduct of the evaluation, meets as needed during the process, delegates tasks, or assigns duties to members or others.
The Evaluation Committee usually consists of the following members:
• Q-responsible of the evaluated unit
• Representative of the institute or program management (if available)
• Representative of the teaching staff
• Representative of the mid-level academic staff
• Representative of the students13

Based on the overarching questions, the concept defines which criteria are to be examined, which data are needed, and which collection methods and instruments are required. 3.3.4
Timeline
Upon completion of the evaluation process design, the Evaluation Committee defines and transparently communicates the timeline and procedures.
Normally, an SPE process extends over two to three semesters – depending also on whether the preparation and concept phases are counted in the process duration. The timeline should only be changed and adjusted in exceptional cases after being set.
3.4
Communication
Comprehensive and transparent communication before, during, and at the conclusion of the SPE processes ensures acceptance and support by all stakeholder groups involved. By the end of the concept phase and after approval of the evaluation concept, the Evaluation Committee informs about the purpose, key elements, and timetable of the evaluation process.
During the process, it is useful to communicate the start and completion of individual steps (conducting surveys, drafting the final report), as well as the conclusion of the process and the key results (see ch. 3.8).
Communication can occur through various channels (e.g. website of the evaluation unit, in courses, email newsletters, flyers, posters).
The Evaluation Committee coordinates communications together with teaching staff members and, if applicable, representatives of the student body.

Data Collection and Analysis as per the Evaluation Concept
Once the concept phase is complete, the SPE process is carried out in accordance with the evaluation concept and its timeline. The Evaluation Committee is responsible for communication, supervises the timeline, and coordinates each step. It analyzes and discusses—with other members of the evaluation unit as needed (e.g. in a workshop)—the results before documenting them in the final report and action plan.
Faculty WiMa_Q as well as the Dept. QSE in VR Quality provide support and advice to evaluation units as needed.

**1. Introduction and Fundamentals**  
Quality assurance and quality development (QSE) have been seen as a central element in all university areas at the University of Bern not only since the implementation of the Higher Education Promotion and Coordination Act (HFKG) of 2011. With the establishment of a holistic quality assurance system encompassing all university performance areas, processes are systematized and suitable measures for QSE are developed and implemented in all university areas. The aim is to continuously further develop the quality of university services and to build a quality culture.

The basis for revising existing, or developing new, QSE measures is the quality strategy of the University of Bern.

**1.1 Purpose of These Guidelines**  
This document focuses on the core university tasks of teaching, research, continuing education, and services. The guidelines are addressed to the Quality Officers (Q-Beauftragten), academic staff for quality (WiMaQ), and all persons responsible for QSE of the core tasks in the faculties, the strategic centers, the centers with a special mandate (summarized below as “university centers”), and the central area. In the field of tension between disciplinary, faculty, and university-wide interests, the maxim is: “As differentiated as necessary for disciplinary reasons, as uniform and comparable as possible.” In this way, the specific needs and traditions of different disciplines are considered on the one hand, and the necessary comparability on the other.

Based on the university quality strategy, the QSE guidelines describe the principles of QSE at the University of Bern for the core university tasks and show how the various elements of QSE are operationalized (organization, communication, etc.). They primarily define the framework conditions for the evaluation of teaching, research, continuing education, and services. They serve as a basis for the faculties to formulate and further develop their faculty-specific guidelines for the QSE of the core university tasks.

---

**1.2 Fundamentals and Principles of These Guidelines**  
The Federal Act on the Promotion and Coordination of Universities in Switzerland (Higher Education Promotion and Coordination Act, HFKG) defines the requirements for universities and ensures, through institutional accreditation, that universities develop their own quality assurance systems and conduct quality assurance and development according to national and international criteria. The law provides the necessary foundations for a competitive, permeable, and high-quality higher education area in Switzerland.

1 For the university's central area there is a separate guideline document.  
2 For the strategic centers and centers with a special mandate, the QSE guidelines of the faculty to which the center is organizationally affiliated generally apply. In addition, they have implemented their own QSE mechanisms.

In accordance with Art. 30, para. 1a of the HFKG, an institutional accreditation requirement is that the university has a quality assurance system which, among other things, ensures the quality of the core tasks of teaching, research, and services. In implementing this requirement, the standards and guidelines for quality assurance in the European Higher Education Area (ESG) as well as the principles of the university’s evaluation regulations are taken into account. The guidelines for the QSE of the core university tasks contribute to meeting these requirements. Based on the legal foundations, they pursue the following principles:  
- Alignment with the principle of high-quality research and good teaching  
- Ensuring a competitive scientific institution  
- Securing and further developing a high quality awareness and thus establishing a university quality culture  
- Involvement and responsibility of all university members according to their competencies  
- Consistency with the tasks, intents, and needs of the faculties, taking into account the diversity of disciplines and faculty cultures

**1.3 Governance and Reporting**  
The central elements of quality assurance and quality development follow the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle (Figure 1). The PDCA cycle describes a continuous improvement process, in which findings flow into quality planning, thus creating a feedback loop.

- **Plan**: The goals for the core university tasks are set in the quality strategy, in the faculty strategies, or in the performance mandates to the university centers. The actions derived from this for goal achievement are listed in the University Action Plan as well as in the action plans of the faculties and the action plan of the strategic centers, based on the University Action Plan.
- **Do**: The responsible university units implement the actions for the core university tasks set out in the action plans.
- **Check**: In order to check to what extent the goals have been achieved, meaningful, QSE-relevant indicators are defined.
- **Act**: In annual operational discussions, the University Management reviews whether and to what extent the faculties have achieved the goals set in their action plans. The results of the review form the basis for updating the action plans. New measures or their adjustments can take account of changes in the framework conditions, such as changes in faculty strategies.

---

**1.4 QSE Organization**  
QSE at the University of Bern is designed to be participatory and is carried by all university members through delegated participation in committees (commissions, committees, etc.) or direct involvement in the development, improvement, and review of services (feedback, exchange, etc.).

The university Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission) advises the University Management on all QSE matters. The faculties, the central area, the Association of Lecturers (Oberer Mittelbau), the Association of Assistants (Unterer Mittelbau), and the students each have a Q-Officer who is a member of the university-wide QSE Commission. In the faculties, the Q-Officers together with the WiMaQ are responsible for the implementation of QSE. The faculties organize QSE in a faculty-specific way. Ideally, faculty QSE committees exist where the subject areas are represented by faculty members (Q-Responsible [Department XY]) and status groups (Q-Responsible [Status XY]). For specific topics, Q-Responsibles of the university centers (Q-Responsible [Center XY]) may also be involved.

The Accreditation Working Group (AKKRED), which operates independently from the QSE Commission, supports the University Management, the central area, the faculties, and the strategic centers in the planning and review of the implementation of the university strategy. In cooperation with the organizational units of the central area, AKKRED annually reviews and updates the University Action Plan as specified by the University Management. AKKRED also supports the faculties with the annual updates of their action plans as well as strategic centers with the annual update of the action plan for strategic centers.

**2. Guidelines for the Evaluation of Teaching**  
The development of teaching, and in particular of study programs or courses of study, is a central responsibility of the faculties and is of primary interest to students, the Canton of Bern as the sponsor of the University of Bern, and the public. Evaluations support the improvement and further development of teaching. They serve the discussion of quality and provide information needed for steering. As recommended by the experts of the Quality Audits and institutional accreditation, the external perspective (e.g., the view of alumni or employers) should increasingly complement internal analyses. The type, scope, and frequency of evaluation are adapted to the purpose of the evaluation.

Furthermore, the evaluation and selection of professors in structural and appointment procedures is of crucial importance for the QSE in teaching.

---

**2.1 Teaching Culture at the University of Bern: High Quality of Teaching and Learning**  
At the University of Bern, high quality of teaching means research-based and varied teaching, which is based on the academic competence of the teaching staff and meets high didactic standards. Students at all qualification levels should be introduced in a partnership-oriented, dialogue-oriented teaching and learning process to creative, critical and interdisciplinary thinking and prepared to assume responsible economic and social roles.

Learning outcomes are defined for study programs, courses of study, and teaching events. Teaching and examination methods are aligned with the learning outcomes. High-quality infrastructure and easy access to modern media are ensured.

When designing teaching, care is taken to ensure the coherence of the course of study, the transitions between study levels and the bridges from school to university and from university to working life. Transparency regarding performance requirements and processes in teaching helps students to organize their studies meaningfully and helps young academics to prepare optimally for an academic career.

---

**2.2 Evaluation of Degree Programmes and Study Programs**  
Through evaluation, the quality of the study offerings (disciplinary, inter-, transdisciplinary Bachelor’s and Master’s degree programs and study programs, Graduate Schools, MAS, and continuing education courses) is critically reviewed. The aim is to improve teaching by identifying strengths and weaknesses. The evaluation of measures related to offerings, students, and teaching staff and the results obtained enable the university to demonstrate to students, supervisory authorities, and society that it is effectively and efficiently implementing the further development of teaching and the promotion of young talent.

All study programs and degree courses offered at the University of Bern are regularly evaluated independently and with the involvement of external reviews. Ideally, an evaluation cycle of seven years is aimed for, from which deviations can be made for justified exceptions. The faculties create binding evaluation plans accordingly.

For study programs and courses offered at the Medical Faculty (sometimes jointly with the Faculty of Science) and Vetsuisse Faculty, which are evaluated and accredited under the Medical Professions Act (MedBG) by the Swiss Agency for Accreditation and Quality Assurance (AAQ) or other external bodies, specific regulations apply; these are set out in the relevant faculty QSE guidelines.

For study program evaluations, where possible, data from the latest BFS graduate survey is taken into account. The overarching assessment of the program's examinations is also part of these evaluations.

Details are regulated by the university guide for the evaluation of study programs and courses at the University of Bern. The standardized processes for evaluation of study programs/courses are regularly reviewed and further developed.

---

**2.3 Evaluation of Courses and Examinations**  
The evaluation of courses (consisting of intermediate evaluation and regulated final evaluation) and the evaluation of examinations are intended to allow instructors to take the perspective of students into account for the further development of their teaching. The goal is to design teaching-learning scenarios, including examinations, that enable the achievement of learning outcomes (improvement-oriented evaluation). In addition, the aggregated results of the final evaluation and the evaluation of examinations ensure the quality of teaching (accountability-oriented evaluation). To meet both purposes, a framework for course evaluation (including evaluation of examinations) has been developed in a joint process with the faculties. The final evaluation and the evaluation of examinations using standardized instruments are carried out by the Department for Course Evaluation (Fachstelle LVE) of the Vice-Rectorate for Quality and Sustainable Development in cooperation with the faculties. The use of intermediate evaluation is available to instructors as an additional tool and is highly recommended. Faculties are free to use additional evaluation instruments and methods and include these in their faculty QSE guidelines.

The following university-wide minimum requirements apply:

- **Evaluation Criteria**  
  The evaluation of courses and examinations follows fixed criteria set out in a model. Thresholds for the classification of evaluation results are defined based on evidence. These criteria, thresholds, and classifications are set out in the framework for course evaluation.

- **Frequency**  
  Each faculty creates a binding evaluation plan that ensures all courses of all institutes/departments as well as 50% of all examinations are evaluated at least once every three years. The Quality Officers are responsible for compliance with the evaluation plan. For instructors in certain qualification positions (e.g., assistant professorships), evaluation is mandatory. It is also mandatory for courses of new instructors and for courses that were rated insufficient in the previous semester.

  Additional evaluations of courses and examinations are possible and can be reported to the Department for Course Evaluation. Examinations are also comprehensively evaluated as part of the study program evaluation.

- **Evaluation Tools**  
  For final and intermediate evaluations as well as for evaluations of oral and written examinations, standardized instruments are used, developed by the Department for Course Evaluation in cooperation with the faculties (see framework document). Faculties may use further tools (such as focus group discussions, self-reports) for course evaluation. For evaluating other types of examinations (besides oral and written), it is recommended to use alternative data collection and evaluation methods. The standardized evaluation tools and procedures for evaluating courses and examinations are regularly reviewed and further developed.

- **Handling Results**  
  Faculties take action for courses that students have rated as excellent or as insufficient. These measures are defined in the framework for course evaluation and in the faculty QSE guidelines.

Further details on the evaluation of courses and examinations can be found in the framework document for course evaluation.

**3. Guidelines for the Evaluation of Research**  
Science is based on integrity. The University of Bern grants researchers great freedom to foster the search for new knowledge and the expansion of the boundaries of knowledge. However, the freedom of researchers is not absolute; they must adhere to ethical and moral values, laws, and regulations, maintain scientific integrity, and correctly handle the funds, assets, and data entrusted to them. The University Management is very concerned that research at Bern continues to be honest and fair. The regulations and instructions on good scientific practice can be found on the University of Bern homepage and are part of employment contracts. In addition, the University offers the opportunity for training in research integrity to sensitize its students and employees.

The University of Bern is aware that the assessment and selection of its professors in appointment procedures (Art. 63, UniV) are of decisive importance for quality assurance and development in research. For this, it consistently relies on the strategy for the promotion of young academics and on transparent and fair appointment procedures.

In addition to securing good scientific practice and high-quality appointment procedures, the University of Bern regards reflection on research performance as central. The evaluation of research achievements helps to identify strengths and weaknesses and provides the basis for recognizing development opportunities and initiating changes.

---

**3.1 Research Evaluation**  
Through research evaluation, the University of Bern aims to achieve transparency regarding the services provided, to prepare decision aids for medium- and long-term planning, and to use the results for quality development in research. It is important that research evaluations are conducted within the framework of a carefully supported concept and, especially regarding usefulness and the use of resources, adhere to the principles of good evaluation practice.

The University of Bern orients itself in the development of guidelines for research evaluation by the recommendations of the Swiss Science Council (SWR) and the recommendations of swissuniversities. The University of Bern refrains from the automatic allocation of resources based on evaluations. It respects the cultures of the disciplines and gives the faculties the necessary leeway to adapt key figures and procedures to the specific needs of the disciplines in bottom-up processes within the framework of university regulations. The University considers this the key to successful reflection on research achievements, which are accepted by researchers and which correspond to the goals and regulations of the University and its understanding as a learning organization.

---

**3.2 Procedures and Requirements in Research Evaluation**  
The need for quantitative and qualitative information about research performance determines the evaluation of research. Research activity includes both the publication output and the entire research process. Subjects of research evaluation are the faculties, departments, institutes, competence centers, research priorities, and other units of the university.

- **3.2.1 Quantitative Methods and Monitoring**  
  The Vice-Rectorate for Research and Innovation at the University of Bern4 systematically collects data on third-party funding, promotion of young researchers, knowledge transfer, and collaborations/networks6 in addition to publication output5 (and possible bibliometric findings in consideration of the DORA Declaration) and provides this to the units as quantitative information for self-reflection and as monitoring.

  The quantitative key figures can be discussed during strategic discussions and included as indicators in faculty action plans. They are also incorporated as information into structural reporting procedures and, in addition to strategic considerations and external input, serve as a basis for discussion and decision-making. For the mapping of research activities in structural reporting processes, at a minimum information on bibliometrics, third-party funding, collaborations, and promotion of young academics are included. Further key figures can be added at the request of the units and in consultation with the data-processing offices.

- **3.2.2 Qualitative Methods and Peer Reviews**  
  Furthermore, research performance of the units is regularly evaluated by external peers within the framework of clear questions. The focus here is on determining the current status, questions about sharpening the profile and development opportunities, the evaluation of current structures, or the evaluation of research activities and results. The University of Bern considers it useful to conduct an external review, for instance, as preparation for new appointments or structural reporting processes. External reviewers are also provided for as voting members of the commissions in structural and appointment procedures. For the assessment of scientific work in research, the following qualitative indicators should be considered: research priorities of the unit; assessment of development goals and opportunities; publications (quality, originality, methodology, impact, etc.); third-party funding (sources and types); national/international, disciplinary/interdisciplinary, university/non-university collaboration/projects (networks, collaborations but also long-term projects); relation between research and teaching; contributions to university self-governance; recognition through awards, prizes, memberships, invitations, calls, review activities, and consulting activities; conditions and achievements in supporting young researchers (administrative burden on young researchers, opportunities for independent research/involvement in research, opportunities to teach independently); transfer of knowledge to society and the economy. An external review generally comprises a self-assessment and an external evaluation, both in the form of a short report.

  The evaluated unit/faculty proposes to the Vice-Rectorate for Research and Innovation a list of possible external reviewers. For an external evaluation, care must be taken to ensure that usually at least three experts (or, in the case of smaller disciplines, accordingly fewer) answer and assess the questions, and that the university’s guidelines on conflicts of interest are observed. The evaluated parties have access to the expert report and may comment on its content. The faculty adopts the final report with findings, recommendations, and measures. The University Management can review the process if needed.

[The OE] forms a peer group of four to five people who can contribute different perspectives on the thematic aspects and questions. The person acting as chair of the group is also appointed [by the OE]. After receipt of the self-evaluation report, this person organizes a virtual preparation for all experts, during which first impressions of the report are exchanged, responsibilities within the group are planned, and information and documents to be provided by the university are named. They also coordinate the planning of the on-site visit (identifying any missing interlocutors or groups, agreement on thematic responsibilities and accountabilities, the sequence of discussions if necessary, and similar matters). In addition, there will be a preparatory meeting of the entire expert group on the evening before the on-site visit, during which procedures and the content of discussions are discussed in detail and responsibilities and any open questions can be clarified.  
During the preparation period, the group is in contact with the Q-responsible person [of the OE] and coordinates with them regarding dates, rooms, and conditions for the site visit.  
4.3 Kick Off  
The process officially begins with a joint (virtual) kick-off meeting of the peer group and the management [of the OE] and its department heads as well as the responsible Q-leads.  
During the event, the purpose, objectives, and questions as well as organizational matters such as scheduling, site visits, and roles and responsibilities in the peer review are defined.  
4.4 On-Site Visit  
During the on-site visit [at the OE], the experts speak with various employees and persons/groups who work in the departments [of the OE]. Together with the written information from the self-evaluation report and other documents, the group should come to assessments regarding the evaluation aspects. Sufficient time is provided to reflect together on findings and to prepare the evaluation report. In a closing discussion at the end of the on-site visit, the group of experts presents preliminary findings.

1. Introduction

The self-evaluation report is an integral component of the peer review process for quality assurance and development (QSE) of the organizational units (OU) of the university's central administration (CA). The assignment to carry out the self-evaluation followed by peer review is given by the Q-Representative of the CA. The report is prepared by the Q-Responsible person of the OU in collaboration with the department heads and serves as the basis for the subsequent assessment of the OU by external reviewers.
The report serves to reflect on the fulfillment and relevance of the core tasks of the OU according to its service description and includes considerations on the further development of existing processes and structures or the establishment of new ones. In addition, the report is oriented towards the sub-strategies and functional strategies relevant to the OU from the university’s Strategy 2030 and the review criteria of the Swiss Agency of Accreditation and Quality Assurance (AAQ) for institutional reaccreditation. The scope of the report document is ten to a maximum of 15 pages (plus appendices). The content is to be formulated clearly and concisely. The final report (PDF) must be submitted electronically by the OU’s Q-Responsible person to the chairperson of the Peer Group by March 31 at the latest.

The coverage of topic areas a – g using some of the pre-formulated or additional questions is mandatory. What does “quality” mean in the higher education context  
and specifically at the University of Bern?  
I would clearly say: At a university, quality is no different  
from skilled craftsmanship. When we hire a craftswoman  
or craftsman, we expect them to be well-trained and to  
complete tasks competently. Likewise, everything done  
at a university must meet very high standards, and  
fulfillment of these standards must be verifiable. Students,  
researchers, staff—as well as the public and policymakers— 
have a right to this. The university is not only subject to  
legislation but is also largely funded by taxpayers.  
“Everything that is done at the university must meet high standards.”

12  
This means that society also has a right to the highest  
possible quality. The drive is to fulfill this claim, so we  
work continuously on quality at all levels of the university’s  
highly complex system.  
This system, with its diversity of competencies, is already  
emphasized in the guiding principle of the University’s  
2018–2021 quality strategy: “Quality assurance and  
development is a complex system and a culture that  
penetrates the entire university.” What is the complexity  
of this system, and what challenges and tasks does that  
create for the university?  
The guiding principle stresses that quality cannot be  
produced at the push of a button; rather, longer processes  
are necessary. It takes a lot of cooperation throughout  
the university to foster a quality culture. It is important

constant measurement, but about learning to look  
closely. Examinations and assessments are not an end  
in themselves; rather, their results should feed back and  
lead to adjustment or changes. This is called closing  
feedback loops. Simply put: it’s about improvement—which  
does not mean what came before was bad. Ultimately,  
everything we check in quality assurance should lead to  
development.  
With institutional accreditation, the University demonstrates  
that it has an excellent quality assurance system.  
Quality is like a virtue.

Initial Situation and Objectives

The Communication Concept for University Quality Assurance and Development (QSE) – in short Communication Concept QSE – aims to comprehensively and adequately inform all university members about measures, procedures, results, the significance and meaningfulness of university QSE, and to involve them in its development. Key documents and regulations regarding QSE are public and known to all staff and students.

Communication on QSE is an essential part of the university’s quality culture. It supports the internal university culture of discussion through direct and active engagement with specific groups via UniBE’s communication channels and via the everyday work of the QSE department in Q-committees and channels to the Q-Community. Regular information is provided on the objectives, procedures, results and best practices in QSE; exchange and networking are promoted, and the rationale, purpose, and added value of quality development for students and staff are made visible.

The communication concept is based on the following fundamentals:
- Strategy 2030 of Universität Bern
- Quality Strategy 2022
- Report on the Institutional Accreditation procedure UniBE
- Institutional Accreditation Guidelines

Implementation of the QSE communication concept is carried out in collaboration with the vice-rectorates, the faculties, the university centers, as well as the Department for Communication and Marketing (AKM).

For specific QSE procedures and projects—such as institutional accreditation, evaluation and accreditation of degree programs, or service certifications—additional communication measures can be defined based on the quality strategy and the present communication concept.

Target Groups

QSE communication is addressed to students, professors, leadership personnel, as well as all further QSE-responsible persons in the faculties, university centers, and central administration, as well as to the public.

a. Students

The education of students, and thus teaching, is a core university task, making students the most important target group for communication. They bear witness to Universität Bern's commitment to teaching and are its reference group. As students and alumni, they are ambassadors of the University: they convey the institution's image as a place of education, a teaching university, and for excellent teaching—even after graduation as alumni and as future employees and employers. According to Strategy 2030, quality assurance and development is one of seven functional strategies that are of central importance for the functioning of the university. In this context, the University of Bern uses the quality assurance and development system to continuously improve its performance. The faculties formulate their contribution to the realization of the functional strategies as well as their specific goals through faculty strategies. Central components of the quality management system are the quality strategy and action plans at both university and faculty levels for their implementation.

The University of Bern’s Quality Strategy 2022 defines the framework for quality assurance and development (QSE) measures and demonstrates the functioning of the quality management system. In doing so, it ensures the sustainable further development of the quality management system and emphasizes values and the university’s understanding of its quality culture. The university’s QSE is based on a lived quality culture, efficient governance as well as measures and processes for implementing QSE.

Bern’s QSE is designed as a participative system, integrated into the existing management systems. QSE is designed as an open, dynamic system that is continuously developed in terms of content. This allows the University of Bern, through guided self-reflection – involving external expertise if necessary – to flexibly adapt to external circumstances and developments. The quality assurance system ensures the implementation of university strategy and the development of the University of Bern.

All areas of the University of Bern are regularly evaluated. In this way, the quality of university services is continuously monitored, and a shared quality culture is developed, which at the same time respects the individuality of the diverse teaching and research cultures of the faculties and their autonomy. The maxim applies, "As differentiated as (academically) necessary, as uniform (and comparable) as possible."

Quality Culture  
Quality culture consists of structural-formal and organization-psychological elements (Sonntag and Sattler 2017). Structural-formal elements are found at the normative, strategic, and operational levels of quality assurance. These are in particular regulations, instructions, as well as guidelines and specific evaluation instruments of the Central QSE area for teaching and research. Central organization-psychological elements for the development of a common quality culture are leadership understanding, participation, communication, commitment, engagement, and sense of responsibility.

The QSE at the University of Bern is strongly participative in design. All members of the university participate through their work in committees such as commissions and boards, or guide it through their involvement in development, improvement, and review of work (feedback, exchange, etc.). Care is taken to keep processes lean in order to reduce the workload of faculty representatives and also to ensure high participation of university members in the process over the long term.

The QSE System  
To formulate, achieve, and maintain the highest quality targets, the University of Bern is oriented towards the quality cycle (Deming Cycle, PDCA cycle), as shown in Figure 1. The relevant objectives are primarily found in Strategy 2030, in the performance mandate of the Government Council to the University of Bern for the years 2022-2025, the institutional accreditation process report, as well as in the functional strategies (Appendix 1). Quality control is the part of the quality assurance system that defines and implements measures to achieve quality objectives. The measures for implementing the quality objectives are recorded in the University Action Plan and the Faculty Action Plans. Using the Faculty Action Plans and the annual strategy discussions with the faculties and centers, it is checked whether the set goals have been achieved. The information obtained from analyses and evaluations flows back into planning, closing the control loop.

3

This leads to process optimizations and measures that improve the structure. Quality control also includes quality documentation. This involves transparent documentation of efforts to improve quality and of the measures taken, as well as adaptations to processes. This provides proof, for example through accreditation, that quality requirements are met.

The University Action Plan lists all measures of the university’s QSE and thus is the most comprehensive document for QSE measures. Together, the University Action Plan and Faculty Action Plans make all university-wide as well as faculty- and center-specific measures of quality assurance and development visible and serve as strategic monitoring tools for the implementation of Strategy 2030 and the faculty strategies. The action plans are structured along the four pillars of the university: Tasks, Personnel, Cross-sectional Topics, Resources, as well as the category Governance.

Measures from the University Action Plan that fall under the responsibility of the faculties or centers are listed in the Faculty Action Plans. The measures from the University Action Plan set the minimum requirements for implementation. The Faculty Action Plans specify how the measures falling within faculty responsibilities are implemented in a faculty- and center-specific manner. To implement the faculty strategies, the Faculty Action Plans are updated based on the faculty strategies’ goals with additional measures.

With the Quality Strategy 2022, the University Action Plan, and the Faculty Action Plans, the University of Bern has the necessary instruments to develop the desired unified quality culture and to set priorities in implementation. The jointly defined values and goals promote the quality awareness of university members and shared values concerning quality. Everyone who researches, teaches, studies, and works at the University of Bern, as well as all organizational units and committees, share responsibility for the quality of university performance.

Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission)  
Quality assurance and development (QSE) is designed to be participatory and is supported by all university members, either through participation in committees or direct involvement in the development, improvement, and evaluation of performance (feedback, exchange, etc.).  
The overall framework of the QSE organization is formed by the Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (QSE Commission), which advises the university management on all matters of quality assurance and quality development. Each faculty, the central administration, the intermediate staff, and the students have a Q representative, who is a member of the university-wide QSE Commission. In the faculties, the Q representatives are supported in their work by scientific staff Q and/or Q assistants and/or Q officers. The Q representatives, if existing, chair faculty-level QSE committees in which the Q officers serve as the link to the departments and institutes of the faculties and promote QSE activities there.

With this award, the University acknowledges and honors the commitment of its instructors to outstanding teaching.

ALL contributes to improving the quality of teaching, promoting appreciation for excellent teaching, and advancing the development of good teaching at the University of Bern as a whole. In addition, it especially provides early career researchers with the opportunity to also stand out in teaching.

The criteria, selection process, and form of the award are outlined in the “Concept for the Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching” (accessible internally).

Objective

In today's world, where education and knowledge are of crucial importance, instructors play a central role. They not only impart knowledge, but also serve as mentors who inspire, encourage, and support students. Outstanding courses can have a lasting impact on students’ lives and professional careers. In recognition of this significance, the University of Bern honors courses that have been evaluated as excellent and highlights them as exemplary. 
In addition, early career researchers are given the opportunity to profile themselves in teaching and to provide official confirmation of positively evaluated courses when applying for jobs.

Procedure
The faculties or institutes decide which courses will be evaluated each semester (see framework concept). (For now, evaluations from the Medical Faculty are not taken into account, as it uses a different form of evaluation.)
The Teaching Evaluation Office (Dept. QSE, Vice-Rectorate for Quality and Sustainable Development) evaluates the selected courses.
After conducting and analyzing all evaluations, the Teaching Evaluation Office nominates, around mid-March for the fall semester (HS) and mid-September for the spring semester (FS), those instructors whose courses meet the established criteria.
The faculties review the nominations and inform the Teaching Evaluation Office of the awarded courses / instructors.
Faculties access the award template via the university’s internal area, complete it with the relevant information, and print the diploma. The diploma is signed by the Dean and given to the respective individuals.
The Teaching Evaluation Office publishes the awarded courses / instructors on the website.

Criteria
To be nominated for ALL, a course must achieve an “excellent” overall result in the final evaluation. Decisive for the classification as “excellent” are the results in the categories “Learning Progress” and “Satisfaction.”

Since HS22, the threshold for the “excellent” category has been set at ≥ 5.7. More information is available here.

In addition to this university-wide criterion, faculties can define individual criteria for awarding ALL:

Additional ALL Criteria by Faculty

Phil-Hist Faculty has the following additional criteria since HS22. In addition to an overall grade of at least 5.7, courses must achieve the following minimum grades for the two indicators of Learning Progress and Satisfaction:
Courses with 1 – 10 survey participants: at least 5.65
Courses with 11 – 20 survey participants: at least 5.55
Courses with 21 – 30 survey participants: at least 5.45
Courses with 31 or more survey participants: at least 5.35
For courses with fewer than 15 evaluation forms, the institute is asked whether the course did not actually have significantly more participants. For 6 or fewer evaluation forms, the institute is asked if awarding the recognition is acceptable. Courses with a lower level of instructor involvement (e.g., research colloquia, lecture series) are excluded from ALL.

Phil-Hum Faculty has had no additional criteria since HS22

Phil-Nat Faculty has the following additional criteria since HS22. Additional regulations (https://www.philnat.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/fak_naturwis/content/e17097/e95924/e258233/e1702458/2025_05_Anhang4_ZusatzregelungenALL_final_ger.pdf) apply in special cases.

RW Faculty has had no additional criteria since HS22

Theology Faculty has had the following additional criteria since HS22. Of all courses evaluated as excellent, the best 5 are given awards.
Excursions, societies and lecture series, as well as courses with fewer than 3 participants, are excluded.
For courses with 3 to 6 participants, ALL is only awarded if the majority of participants took part in the evaluation.

1. Introduction  
1.1 Background and Principles  
The University of Bern is required by national legislation to ensure and document quality assurance and development (QSE) for its core activities: teaching, research, and continuing education.1  
Based on this, the interdisciplinary and/or interfaculty centers of the University of Bern with a strategic focus or special mandate are each subjected to a self-evaluation process in preparation for new mandates. Evaluations2 are an integral part of the QSE system at the University of Bern.  
For the planning, design, implementation, and analysis of evaluations, the centers, in coordination with the faculties organizationally assigned to them according to their mandate, are responsible and should follow university and faculty standards and guidelines.3  
The self-evaluations of the centers are primarily a QSE instrument that enables systematic reflection on the specific profile and the quality of research and any other services at the level of the evaluating units from different perspectives. They document and process quantitatively and qualitatively collected data and information on the content concepts and their relevance and strategic significance, as well as on structures and organization. Thus, evaluations are intended to enable the best possible performance and profile sharpening of the centers or the university institutes involved in them by helping to further develop and, if necessary, correct content, methods, and conditions.  
Furthermore, the evaluations are important for the development and positioning of the University as a scientific and research institution as well as for faculties, departments, and institutes. In strategy discussions of the university management (UL) with the centers and faculties, or in structural report procedures, evaluation results can play a role as planning elements.  

1.2   This Document  
To support those responsible for QSE in the centers and the organizationally assigned faculties, the following recommendations and information are provided for conducting a self-evaluation. The document is based on the evaluation processes conducted in the centers and the experiences gained, and replaces the Guide for the Self-Evaluation of Competence Centers at the University of Bern, approved in March 2013.  
The revised guide describes the framework and objectives of evaluation procedures for centers, their process flow, and the responsibilities in the evaluation process. The information and recommendations serve as guidelines and, except for elements marked as "mandatory," are not binding. However, they should be taken into account wherever possible to ensure that the evaluation processes can be successfully completed.  

2. General Conditions and Organization of the Self-Evaluations  
Each center management is responsible for the scheduled execution of an evaluation. The timing of an evaluation follows the performance period established in the respective performance mandate. Clearly defined, cyclical performance periods ensure transparency and planning security with regard to the resources to be made available for each evaluation process.  
A completed and positively assessed self-evaluation by the university management is a prerequisite for the issuing of a new performance mandate and the associated allocation of financial resources.  
Evaluation procedures are fundamentally participatory. Thus, it is possible to discuss and further develop research and other activities and infrastructural conditions, based on the analyses carried out during the evaluation, in the context of the requirements formulated in the respective performance mandates and the scientific, university, and societal requirements, together with all stakeholders and with external support.  
From these premises arise the following, sometimes overlapping, steps in which an evaluation procedure typically takes place:  
1. Preparation and planning  
2. Formation of the evaluation committee including external reviewers  
3. Development of the evaluation concept and timeline  
4. Data collection and analysis as per the evaluation concept  
5. Preparation and approval of the final report and action plan  
6. Evaluation of the report and formulation of the new mandate  
7. Follow-up reporting  
8. Communication  

3. The Self-Evaluation Process  
3.1 Preparation and Planning  
As a rule, the scientific staff member (WiMa) in the Department of Quality Assurance and Development (QSE) in the Vice-Rectorate (VR) Quality initiates the preparation of the evaluation process about 16 months before the expiration of the current performance period defined in the respective performance mandate. Subsequently, the center management or the person responsible for quality (Q-Responsible) clarifies the key procedural issues with the QSE Dept. (see checklist in Appendix 1).  
Furthermore, the center management or the Q-Responsible prepares a timeline and drafts an evaluation concept (see Ch. 3.3) and seeks suitable members for the evaluation committee (see Ch. 3.2). They also clarify whether and to what extent financial resources are necessary for carrying out the evaluation.  
The center management decides how to inform the members of the relevant center about the upcoming evaluation (see Ch. 3.8).  

3.2 Evaluation Committee incl. External Reviewers  
The term "evaluation committee" is generic. Centers are free to designate the committee they convene for the purpose of evaluation as they wish.  
To fully meet the requirements and objectives of the self-evaluation of centers, it is desirable to incorporate a variety of perspectives on the subjects to be evaluated. Therefore, all stakeholder groups involved in a center should be represented in an evaluation committee.  
After its establishment, the committee decides on the evaluation concept (see Ch. 3.3) and the associated timeline (see Ch. 3.3.4). It is responsible for the execution of the evaluation, meets as needed throughout the process, and delegates tasks to individual members or other persons as required.  
Typically, the evaluation committee is composed of the following members:  
• Q-Responsible of the respective center  
• Representative of the center management  
• Representative of the academic mid-level staff  
• Representative of administrative and/or technical staff  
• possibly external person(s) (see below)  
• possibly representative of the teaching staff  
• possibly representative of the students  

The WiMa responsible for center evaluations in the QSE Dept. accompanies the procedures in an advisory and supporting capacity. The faculty Q-Representatives and WiMa_Q may be involved in answering specific questions that only pertain to the faculty.  
The university has made the inclusion of external review and expertise in assessing the quality of teaching, research, and career development mandatory. The aim of external review is to check and, if necessary, complement the internal view with an independent perspective, and provide support in interpreting available data. The external perspective4 is ensured either by including one or more peer persons in the evaluation committee or by commissioning one or more external reviews. The decision depends on the questions posed to the external experts. In all cases, early consideration and planning of this aspect is strongly recommended.  

3.3 Evaluation Concept and Timeline  
With the evaluation concept, the evaluation committee establishes important grounds for the upcoming evaluation process. It sets out the strategic goals and guiding questions, individual steps, deadlines, and responsibilities bindingly.  
Based on the main questions, the concept defines the criteria to be examined, the necessary data, and the required collection methods and instruments. Ideally, the document also includes a timeline (see Ch. 3.3.4) and a list of committee members (see Ch. 3.2).  

3.3.1 Criteria for the Evaluation of Centers  
To assess the quality of the centers' research, teaching, and, where applicable, services, as well as their strategic orientation and infrastructural conditions with reference to the requirements and measures set out in the performance mandates, the following criteria are relevant for each self-evaluation:  
• Strategic overall orientation: Positioning in the national and international context; contribution to society; implementation of the performance mandate; possibilities and focus of development (possibly in reference to previous evaluation results)  
• Structures (incl. governance and administration)  
• Research profile: research activities and results considering available resources and strategic relevance; positioning in the national and international context; cooperation; career support; publication strategy  
• Teaching and career support: teaching and learning activities, their outcomes and links to current research; organization of teaching operations; graduate schools and other programs associated with the centers; specific measures and instruments for career support  
• Services (if part of the performance mandate)  
• University-wide cross-cutting themes: sustainability, internationalization, equality and equal opportunities, QSE, communication and related processes and structures  
• Integration and cooperation at the university and faculty level: structural aspects, university and/or faculty action plans  
• Human resources (academic and non-academic staff): focus on research, service and teaching activities; staff profile and development; mobility  
• Financial resources and infrastructure  

These criteria allow all aspects of the performance mandates to be addressed and analyzed specific to the center. Therefore, they may differ from the evaluation criteria of other institutions.  
Prioritization and weighting of the criteria result from the objectives defined in the respective evaluation concept. The criteria are not always distinctly separable, and some of the collected data can naturally be evaluated for several criteria.  

3.3.2 Data and Data Sources  
Based on the prioritization and weighting of the criteria to be evaluated, a decision is made on which data and information should be obtained and analyzed.  
The following list is neither exhaustive nor binding. The respective evaluation committee decides which data and sources promise the best results and can be obtained with a reasonable amount of effort.  
• Fundamental documents: statutes, framework regulations, rules of procedure, performance mandates, strategy papers  
• Additional data collections: structural reports, annual reports, study program evaluations  
• Statistical data and key figures: research key figures & bibliometric data5, personnel statistics6  
• Required data (see Ch. 3.3.3): quantitative and qualitative information and feedback from researchers, lecturers, external reviewers (mandatory, see Ch. 3.2), former center members  

3.3.3 Methods of Data Collection  
Based on the defined evaluation criteria and the necessary data, the evaluation committee decides on the appropriate methods and instruments for collecting data that are not already available.  
The following quantitative and qualitative methods are generally applicable. Their use should depend on the type of data to be collected as well as the target groups:  
• Surveys with questionnaires  
• Focus group discussions  
• Individual interviews  
• Retreats or workshops  

Other methods and hybrid forms are also possible (e.g., representative statements by stakeholder groups that go beyond qualitative comments in surveys).  

3.3.4 Timeline  
Upon completion of the design of the evaluation procedures, the evaluation committees establish the processes and timelines bindingly and communicate them transparently.  

3.4 Data Collection and Analysis  
After the conception phase ends, the evaluation procedures are carried out according to the individual evaluation concept and corresponding timeline. The respective evaluation committee is responsible for communication actions, oversees timelines, and coordinates the individual steps. It analyzes and discusses – possibly with further members of the centers – the evaluation results before these are included in the final report and action plan.  

3.5 Evaluation Report and Action Plan  
The final report is a central element of the self-evaluation. Its quality significantly determines the usefulness and effectiveness of the whole process. It provides a snapshot of the evaluated center’s current situation and shows its development potential. Since the evaluation results also serve as the basis for decisions on new performance mandates, it is essential that the final report contains the most precisely reasoned statements and clearly formulated actions for future development.  

3.5.1 Evaluation Report  
In a brief but meaningful final report, the evaluation committee documents the evaluation methods, data, and results, as well as measures that serve the development of the center and its framework conditions.  
The final report may be prepared as a text document not exceeding 20 pages (excluding appendices) or as a slide presentation with about 15-20 slides. It must be written in German or English and is preferably submitted electronically (in PDF) to the QSE Dept.  
The report must obligatorily contain the following components:  
• List of evaluation committee members and their roles  
• 1-2 page summary, presenting the main outcomes of the respective performance period, especially in the areas of research, teaching, and career support, as well as an indication of any major critical issues discussed in the following report7  
• Results and insights with regard to the evaluation criteria according to the evaluation concept (see Ch. 3.3.1)  
• Action plan (possibly included as an appendix)  
• Separate statement(s) from external reviewers, if these are not members of the evaluation committee as peers (see Ch. 3.2; include as appendix)8  
• Other relevant appendices (tables, statistics, regulations, supplemental information)  

3.5.2 Action Plan  
The aim of evaluation processes is the substantive and strategic further development of the evaluated centers.  
Therefore, it is necessary to derive targeted measures from the collected data and findings and to formulate them as specifically as possible, especially with regard to the new performance mandates to be formulated. In preparation, SWOT analyses can be used, in which the evaluation results are summarized in a matrix and from which the majority of measures to be adopted can be easily deduced (see Appendix 2).  
The involvement of all stakeholder groups represented in the evaluation committees in the measures discussion is a prerequisite for ensuring that all those affected, or at least the majority, consider the actions realistic and meaningful and support them.  
The action plan should take the following aspects into account:  
• Justification of the measures, problem description  
• Name of the measure  
• Responsibility for implementation  
• Implementation period or deadline  
• Priority  
• Resource requirements  
• Impact indicators for assessing the status of implementation  

3.5.3 Approval Procedure  
The evaluation committee agrees on the final version of the final report and the action plan contained in it and, if necessary, submits both to the center management for approval. Afterwards, the dean and the Q-Representative of the organizationally assigned faculty have the opportunity to review and comment on the documents.9 With a note regarding this review, the report documents are preferably submitted electronically to the WiMa responsible for the evaluation procedures in the QSE Dept.  

3.6 Evaluation of Report and New Mandate  
The results of the evaluation processes are evaluated in the QSE Dept. based on the submitted final reports and summarized for the UL. After a positive assessment of the evaluation results and the measures derived from them, the UL instructs the UL staff, in cooperation with the center managements and the QSE Dept., as well as (optionally) the Q-Representatives of the faculties involved, to draft the new performance mandates.  
Where weaknesses or deficiencies are identified with respect to fulfillment of performance mandates or serious structural problems, the QSE Dept. may, in consultation with the Vice-Rector for Quality, develop specific measures for inclusion in the performance mandate together with the center management and/or the evaluation committee. In rare cases and after careful examination, the Vice-Rector for Quality may submit a request to the UL for an external evaluation.  

3.7 Communication  
Transparent communication before, during, and at the conclusion of evaluation processes ensures acceptance and support among all stakeholder groups involved.  
At the latest by the end of the conception phase (see Ch. 3.3), the respective evaluation committee informs about the significance, main elements, and dates of the evaluation process. During the process, it is useful to communicate the start and end of individual steps (conducting surveys, drafting the final report) as well as the completion of the procedure and the main results. Further, the center management informs the mainly involved faculty in an appropriate manner10 about the completion and main results of the self-evaluation.  

3.8 Follow-up Reporting  
The forms and cycles of follow-up reporting are laid out in the performance mandates.  

3.8.1 Strategy Discussions  
As part of the annual strategy discussions, the center management reports to the UL, using a reporting template defined by the UL staff, about the implementation status of the services and measures agreed upon in the performance mandates.  
In the fourth year of each performance period, in which the centers conduct self-evaluations in preparation for new mandates, no strategy discussions take place.  

3.8.2 Follow-up Meeting  
In the first half of the second year of the current performance periods (ideally by the end of the spring semester), the Vice-Rector Quality and the WiMa responsible for the evaluation procedures in the QSE Dept. hold a meeting with the center managements.11 The previous evaluation processes are reviewed, the new mandates and their implementation in QSE-relevant aspects and measures are discussed, and any further QSE-related topics are addressed.  
The focus of the follow-up meetings is not content reporting on various performance areas. It is an open exchange to support the centers' work.

Appendix 2: SWOT Analysis Model (to Ch. 3.5.2)  
SWOT analysis is a standardized instrument applied in organizational development. It is used for determining position and strategy formation and can also be used in a university context. An internal and external perspective is taken on the subject, and various criteria and data are set in relation to each other in a matrix:  
• Strengths: Focus on one's (current) strengths and abilities  
• Weaknesses: Focus on one's (current) weaknesses  
• Opportunities: Outlook on (future) conditions with positive potential  
• Threats: Outlook on (future) conditions with negative potential  

These four categories are set in relation to each other in four directions according to the following scheme. Various combinations and thus strategic objectives are developed.

In the case of center evaluation, the quantitatively and qualitatively obtained results and findings can be assigned to the four categories using the SWOT analysis and sorted by relevance for the center being evaluated. The three to five (the number should be the same in all categories) most important identified strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats are set in relation to each other according to the schema, so that strategic objectives can be formulated. Introduction

The self-report is written by those instructors whose course has been evaluated as falling into the category of “insufficient teaching”. These instructors are asked to comment on the evaluation results from the first final evaluation, the subsequent interim evaluation, and the renewed final evaluation, and to write a corresponding self-report to be submitted to the persons specified by their faculty. If you are unsure whom you should submit the self-report to, please contact the quality representative of your faculty.

 

Objectives of the Self-Report

The self-report is intended to help instructors:

Recognize the strengths and weaknesses in their own teaching;

Be able to respond to the course evaluation results;

Identify areas of action that can be modified or have been modified, and explore possible scope for action;

Point out any need for support or advice.

 

The report captures the most essential elements from the students’ feedback. The opinion of the instructor may differ from that of the students. If this is the case, this assessment can be explained.

 

My comments on the results of my course evaluation in relation to the influencing factors and/or the achievement of the target criteria. Do I share the students’ impression? How can I explain the results? How have the results of the interim evaluation and/or the renewed final evaluation changed? For what reasons?

What were the strengths of my course? Which aspects were judged positively by the students?

Where do I still see a need for improvement in my course based on the students’ feedback?

What specific improvement measures have I implemented or will I implement?

To what extent do I require further support in implementing improvement measures or in the future planning of courses within or outside my faculty (e.g., advice from Hochschuldidaktik & Lehrentwicklung, University of Bern)?

To what extent do I require further advice for interpreting the evaluation results? Trainings and Materials

b  
for implementing sub-strategies within the competence of the University Management (link to the University Action Plan),  
c  
from the minutes of the strategy talks of the previous year.

on the implementation status of the measures in the University Action Plan, the faculties’ action plans, and the Strategic Centers Action Plan for the purpose of:

Standardization of the Evaluation Method  
In the QSE Commission meeting on 09.03.2022, a vote was held regarding the standardization of the method for centrally conducted course evaluations (midterm evaluation, final evaluation). It was decided that these evaluations will henceforth be conducted online using non-personalized links (Losung). This standardization simplifies the entire course evaluation process and makes it less prone to errors. Faculty-specific Additional Regulations for the Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching (ALL)

Unanimously adopted by the faculty QSE commission on 15.05.2025 and approved by the Faculty of Science on 22.05.2025.

1 Introduction
Outstanding achievements in teaching are recognized and valued at the University of Bern. The basis for this recognition is the results of standardized course evaluations. The criteria for classifying a course as "outstanding" were developed by the Vice-Rectorate for Quality and Sustainable Development in cooperation with the faculties. The recognition of outstanding achievements in teaching (ALL) in the form of a diploma is intended as an incentive for good teaching and thus serves to motivate teaching staff to further improve their teaching. Furthermore, this specifically provides the mid-level faculty1 with the opportunity to distinguish themselves in teaching and to present official confirmation of positive achievements when applying for positions.
Further information about the ALL distinction can be found in the university document “Concept for the Recognition of Outstanding Achievements in Teaching (ALL)”. This document explains the university-wide criteria, the general selection process, and the form of recognition (instructions for creating the diploma).
In addition to the university-wide ALL concept, the following describes additional guidelines and regulations that apply specifically to the Faculty of Science.
Within the Faculty of Science, once per year another award for good teaching – the Faculty Teaching Prize – is given. Further information about this can be found in Appendix 3 of the faculty QSE guidelines.

1 This includes full-time teaching staff, assistant professors, lecturers, guest lecturers, senior assistants, academic staff, assistants, postdocs, and associate professors.

Faculty-specific Additional Regulations for ALL Recognition – Faculty of Science

2 Faculty-specific Regulations
Multiple ALL nominations per instructor:
•
If an instructor receives an outstanding evaluation for multiple courses within a semester, these are listed together in a single ALL diploma. An exception applies for courses with multiple instructors.
Multiple instructors per ALL nomination:
•
Only instructors can receive an ALL diploma and be listed on it if they were registered in KSL as co-instructors2 for the respective course and were significantly involved in the organization and execution of the course.
Tutors and other assistants who only performed supporting tasks cannot receive an ALL diploma.
•
For courses assessed as outstanding that had multiple instructors, they are listed together on the ALL diploma (see example on page 3, usually in alphabetical order). Each listed instructor can receive an ALL diploma (several identical diplomas).

3 Procedure
3.1
The LVE Office nominates the courses based on the success level achieved and submits the results to the faculty QSE (WiMaQ) after each semester in the form of an average value table.3
3.2
The faculty QSE (WiMaQ) contacts the main instructors of the nominated courses and clarifies, in the case of several instructors, who was significantly/primarily involved in the organization and execution and should receive the ALL award.
3.3
All nominated instructors then receive their ALL diploma (in German or English) sent by post.
3.4
The faculty QSE (WiMaQ) communicates the awarded courses and names of the instructors to the LVE Office. The Q representative also informs the faculty assembly in a faculty meeting about the ALL nominations for the respective semester.
3.5
The awarded courses and the names of the instructors are published by the LVE Office on the website “https://www.gutelehre.unibe.ch” and by the faculty QSE (WiMaQ) on the faculty QSE website.

2 Exceptions are possible if supported by the responsible program director.
3 Since practica, excursions and block courses may still take place during semester breaks, the final average value table may only be available shortly before or at the beginning of the new semester.

The Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development

Scope  
Status  
Tasks  
Based on Article 36 paragraph 1 letter m of the Law on the University of 5 September 1996 (UniG) as well as Article 21 paragraph 1 letter m, Article 29 paragraph 1 letter b no. 2 and Article 30 of the Statutes of the University of Bern of 7 June 2011 (University Statute, UniSt),  
decrees:  

Art. 1  
These rules of procedure regulate the status, the tasks, and the organization of the Commission for Quality Assurance and Quality Development (hereinafter QSE-Commission).

Art. 2  
1 The QSE-Commission is a standing commission of the University of Bern within the meaning of Article 29 paragraph 1 letter b no. 2 UniSt.  
2 It is administratively assigned to the Vice-Rectorate Quality. This office is responsible for the planning, preparation, and follow-up of commission meetings as well as the drafting of the meeting minutes.

Art. 3  
1 The QSE-Commission advises the university management on matters of quality assurance and quality development.  
2 The QSE-Commission  
  a) prepares foundational documents to ensure quality assurance and quality development at both the university-wide and faculty levels, while adhering to the relevant university, cantonal and national guidelines and taking into account the diversity of subject and faculty cultures,  
  b) issues these rules of procedure and submits them to the Senate for approval,  
  c) advises the university management regarding the conception and further development of the university’s quality assurance system,  
  d) handles further matters concerning university QSE that are assigned by the university management.

Chair  
Representation

Art. 4  
1 The QSE-Commission consists of the following voting members:  
  a) the Vice-Rector for Quality (Chair),  
  b) the Q-Representative of each faculty,  
  c) the Q-Representative of the Association of Lecturers (VDD),

The Swiss Accreditation Council has accredited the University of Bern as a higher education institution without conditions.



With the accreditation decision that the University of Bern received on September 24, 2021, the process of institutional accreditation was able to be completed (further information in the procedural report [https://aaq.ch/download/universitaet-bern-unibe/]).

According to the Federal Act on Funding and Coordination of the Swiss Higher Education Sector [https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2014/691/de], accreditation is a prerequisite for the University of Bern to continue to be designated as a university and to receive the federal funding provided for in the law.

The basis for the positive accreditation decision was a self-assessment report prepared by the University of Bern in 2020 [https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e809/e810/e812/e708931/e715528/e1059275/Selbstbeurteilungsbericht2020_ger.pdf], as well as the review by external experts in spring 2021. With their analyses and assessments, they concluded that the University of Bern has an efficient quality assurance system that covers all areas and processes of the university. Particularly positive mention was made by the experts of the successful participation of all faculties, groups, and central staff in the development, implementation, and realization of the QSE processes.

The accreditation is valid for a period of seven years.

For more information, read the article and the accompanying interview with Prof. Silvia Schroer in the magazine unilink (Issue 03/2021) [https://www.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/content/e809/e810/e812/e708931/e715528/e1153738/Unilink_November_2021_DEF_ger.pdf]. Centers and Cross-Faculty Units

**Strategic Centers**  
**Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics (AEC)**

The Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics was founded in 2011 in order to promote research and teaching in fundamental physics at the University of Bern at a high level. The focus is on experimental and theoretical particle physics, but also includes interdisciplinary activities such as medical applications. It is also strongly involved in public outreach. The center connects the Institute for Theoretical Physics and the Laboratory for High Energy Physics (LHEP) and works together with other members of the science and medical faculties. With over 120 members, the AEC is one of the largest university groups for particle physics in Switzerland and is a strong international player in science and education.  
To the website (https://www.einstein.unibe.ch/index_eng.html)

**ARTORG Center for Biomedical Engineering Research**

At the ARTORG Center, innovative health technology is created through the combination of engineering and medical expertise. Multidisciplinary research groups develop cost-efficient solutions for challenges in preventive medicine, diagnostics, therapy, the management of chronic diseases, as well as rehabilitation and telemedicine. Through inventive spirit, diversity, and teamwork at the heart of the Medical Faculty, ARTORG puts its expertise in the simulation of physiological processes, biomechanics, and intelligent technologies at the service of healthcare professionals, and contributes directly to improved patient care through its own start-ups and industry partners.  
To the website (https://www.artorg.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Bern Center for Precision Medicine (BCPM)**

The Bern Center for Precision Medicine (BCPM) was founded in 2019 on the initiative of the canton, the university, and Insel Gruppe, and is active in science, education, and networking. The center is devoted to precision medicine methods by promoting the research and development of new treatments and therapies. It offers an interdisciplinary platform for researchers and clinicians from various fields and faculties. It trains the next generation of doctors, nurses, and scientists, and realizes the long-term benefits of precision healthcare.  
To the website (https://www.bcpm.unibe.ch/)

**Center for Regional Economic Development (CRED)**

The Center for Regional Economic Development (CRED) is an interfaculty center for research, teaching, and consulting on issues of regional economic development. The center brings together a group of researchers from the research units of economics, entrepreneurship, geography, and tourism who deal with the following research focal points: (1) location dynamics and regional economic policy, (2) tourism, and (3) land policy and real estate. CRED is a scientific partner for regional, cantonal, national, and international public and private entities.  
To the website (https://www.cred.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Center for Space and Habitability (CSH)**

The Center for Space and Habitability (CSH) is tasked with promoting dialogue and interaction between the various scientific disciplines that deal with the formation, discovery, and characterization of other worlds inside and outside our solar system, our search for life in other parts of the universe, and their impact on disciplines beyond the natural sciences. We actively advocate for equality regardless of gender, origin, religion, etc., and engage in dialogue with various target groups.  
To the website (https://www.csh.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Centre for Development and Environment (CDE)**

The CDE is the competence center for sustainable development. In the spirit of engaged and transformative science, we place our research and teaching at the service of a sustainable world. The goal is to develop ways to achieve sustainable development in Switzerland and internationally, and to initiate change processes that are innovative, concrete, and anchored in context. We investigate the interrelationship between natural resources, land use, and ecosystems, their social and political regulation, as well as living conditions and human well-being. At the University of Bern, we are tasked with embedding sustainability university-wide in research and teaching.  
To the website (https://www.cde.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Kompetenzzentrum für Public Management (KPM)**

The Kompetenzzentrum für Public Management at the University of Bern was founded in 2002. It is a nationally and internationally renowned research center in the field of administrative science. As a cross-faculty unit of the Faculty of Business, Economics and Social Sciences and the Faculty of Law, it is committed to interdisciplinary collaboration between the disciplines of law, economics, and political science. The core products of the center are basic research in the field of public institution management, the Master's program Public Management & Policy (PMP), as well as the postgraduate programs Executive Master of Public Administration (Executive MPA) and the CAS "Management und Politik öffentlicher Institutionen" (CeMaP). In addition, services are provided for the public sector in the form of analyses, expert reports, and evaluations.  
To the website (https://www.kpm.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Multidisciplinary Center for Infectious Diseases (MCID)**

The Multidisciplinary Center for Infectious Diseases (MCID) is a strategic center at the University of Bern, founded in 2021 through a generous donation from the Vinetum Foundation. The MCID is dedicated to researching and containing health, social, ethical, and economic risks caused by infectious diseases and is engaged in research, teaching, and public outreach. It brings together top academic researchers from the University of Bern, the Inselspital, and associated institutes. The MCID is organized into seven interconnected scientific discipline clusters, which center on three core activities: the BEready cohort, the BioPreparedness BioBank, and the Ethics and Policy Lab.  
To the website (https://www.mcid.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**Oeschger Centre for Climate Change Research (OCCR)**

The Oeschger Centre for Climate Change Research (OCCR) is a leading climate research center, bringing together researchers from 14 institutes and four faculties. The OCCR conducts interdisciplinary research at the forefront of climate science. The Oeschger Centre was founded in 2007 and is named after Hans Oeschger (1927-1998), a pioneer of modern climate research who worked in Bern. The OCCR has about 300 members. Their research focuses on the impacts of climate change on human beings and ecosystems, and they are heavily involved in studying the social consequences of this unprecedented change.  
To the website (https://www.oeschger.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)

**World Trade Institute (WTI)**

The World Trade Institute (WTI) is a leading academic institution engaged in study, research, and public outreach at an international level on issues of international trade regulation and investment. As a competence center at the University of Bern, we explore the interconnections between law, economics, and political science. We recruit students, researchers, faculty, and staff from around the world to create a diverse culture and stimulating environment. With an established global partner network, WTI stands at the forefront of interdisciplinary research in the field of international trade regulation and investment. We investigate, collect data, and present policy recommendations on how trade is related to issues such as migration, environment/climate change, investment, agriculture/food security, energy, raw materials, and development.  
To the website (https://www.wti.unibe.ch/index_ger.html)
